How this programme works

The four mechanics that do most of the teaching

Executive programmes about AI and analytics share a characteristic failure mode, and it is worth naming at the start because everything on this page is a response to it. The room watches a series of fluent, impressive demonstrations. Everybody agrees that they were impressive. The week is enjoyable, the food is good, and then people return to work able to do very little differently.

What makes this so persistent is that the demonstrations are enjoyable because they are frictionless. The presenter has already met every obstacle and removed it. But the obstacles were not incidental to the learning — they were the learning. The moment where the query returns three times as many rows as you expected, and you have to work out why, is the moment where the understanding actually forms.

So four mechanics exist to put the friction back deliberately. They are not decoration, and they are not team-building. If you take nothing else from this page, take these four.

1 · The four-minute rule

No demonstration runs longer than four minutes before the room does it.

Four minutes is roughly the point at which watching somebody else work stops being orientation and starts being entertainment. Up to that point you are building a mental model of what is about to happen. Past it, you are a spectator, and spectators do not retain much. So after four minutes: hands on keyboards.

This has consequences, and they are worth naming in advance because on Monday morning they will feel like faults rather than choices:

  • Sessions look less polished than a keynote. There is more fumbling, and more visible difficulty, because real work looks like that.
  • Faculty answer more questions they did not plan for, which is uncomfortable for them and useful for you.
  • Less material gets covered than a lecture format would cover.

All three are the price of the design, and all three are worth paying — the alternative is a smoother week that leaves less behind. If a faculty member overruns, the room is entitled to say so out loud. Please do; it is genuinely helpful rather than rude.

2 · Driver and Sceptic

You work in pairs, and the roles swap every day, so that everybody spends half the week in each seat.

The Driver is on the keyboard. They talk to the assistant, run the code, and produce the result.

The Sceptic may not touch the keyboard. Their job is to produce an objection before the result is used — not afterwards, when it has already been written on the wall board and defending it has become a matter of pride.

The Sceptic’s standing questions

  1. What is the grain of this table? One row per what?
  2. How many rows before the join, and how many after?
  3. What period are we comparing to what period, and are they the same length?
  4. Real or nominal? And if real, deflated by what?
  5. Would we have known this at the moment we needed to act?
  6. What had to be true for a row to exist in this file?
  7. If this result is this good, what is wrong with it?

Seven questions. Between them they catch a large share of what goes wrong in commercial analytics, and — this is the part worth noticing — not one of them requires you to be able to write a line of code. They are questions about meaning, not about syntax, which is why they travel so well back to your own desk.

The Sceptic role is not a game and it is not a politeness. It exists because of a specific and fairly recent asymmetry: an assistant is an outstanding generator of analysis and a poor auditor of the question it was handed. Ask it a well-posed question and you will get an excellent answer. Ask it a subtly wrong question and you will get an equally excellent answer to the wrong question, delivered with exactly the same confidence. Nothing in the tool closes that gap. Somebody in the room has to, and pairing is how the habit gets built rather than merely recommended.

By Wednesday most pairs report that the Sceptic seat is harder work than the Driver seat. That is the correct discovery, and it usually arrives as a surprise.

3 · Salted data

The data room carries thirteen deliberate defects, and does not tell you where they are.

You are told the kinds of thing to expect — a join key that silently duplicates rows, a feature that leaks the answer, a structural break a forecast will ignore, a definition that contradicts finance, an entity recorded twice under two names, an extract with a filter on it — and nothing at all about which file holds which. You find that out on Friday afternoon, and not before.

That distinction is deliberate and it is the whole design. Knowing the taxonomy of defects is the transferable part: it is what turns the useless instruction “check the data” into a short list of specific questions you can put to any table anybody hands you, for the rest of your career. Knowing that row 4,812 of one particular CSV is duplicated is worth precisely nothing on Monday week.

The defects are not one per file, and some files are entirely clean. That is deliberate too, and it is the detail people find most surprising. A room that has been told everything is dirty will hunt until it finds something, and finding a defect in a clean file is a worse habit to leave with than missing one in a dirty file — it teaches you to distrust your own instrument. The discipline being built is to run the same handful of checks on everything and let the file tell you what it is.

The underlying reasoning is simple enough. Being wrong in a classroom costs you a moment of mild discomfort among peers who are about to be wrong themselves. Being wrong in a board paper costs considerably more, and it tends to cost it slowly and in public. The instinct that prevents the second is built by experiencing the first, in a setting designed for it.

Three things follow, and they are worth saying plainly before Monday:

  • You will present findings that turn out to be wrong. Everybody does, including faculty when we run these labs ourselves. It is the design working, not a reflection on you.
  • Nobody is corrected in the moment. Tables present, the room listens, and the reveal comes afterwards — because being interrupted mid-sentence teaches embarrassment rather than method.
  • The tables that catch the defects are not the clever ones. They are the ones with the habit. The habit is the deliverable.

4 · The failure logbook

Every table keeps a running log with three columns: what the assistant produced, what was wrong with it, and the question that caught it.

Two entries a day is typical, and it is a low enough bar that nobody has to strain for material. On Friday afternoon every table’s entries are consolidated, deduplicated and read aloud as a single page — the ways the copilot misled us this week — and then sent to the whole cohort.

It costs an hour of facilitation. It is also, reliably, the artefact people forward to their own teams in the week afterwards, and it is the most honest summary available of what the week was actually about.

Two more things that shape the week

The daily one number

Every morning at 09:00, each person writes down one number they now believe about their business, with an interval around it. Ten minutes, no discussion.

Monday’s is sealed in an envelope. Friday’s is written next to it and the two are compared.

For most people the number itself moves only a little, and the interval gets wider. That looks at first like going backwards, and it is not. It is the week working: you now know more about what you do not know, which is the precondition for every good decision made with data. A confident point estimate that turns out to be wrong is a much more expensive object than a range you understood the shape of.

Two-speed labs

Every lab has a Core that everybody finishes and a Stretch for those who move faster, so that nobody is either stranded or idle.

The Stretch task is nearly always some version of “break another table’s result”. That is deliberate on two counts. It turns the strongest people in the room into a teaching resource rather than leaving them bored, and adversarial skill — finding the flaw in a plausible argument — is precisely the thing an assistant cannot supply for you.

A floor technologist handles setup problems throughout the week, so that faculty never have to stop teaching to debug somebody’s installation.

What is expected of you

  • A one-page problem charter, submitted ten days before Monday. It becomes your capstone, so choose something you actually want an answer to.
  • The twenty-minute data-triage diagnostic. It is not a test — it is how we stream the labs and pair people well. Nobody sees anybody’s score.
  • Attendance at the setup clinic if your laptop is managed by your IT department. It is much easier to solve a fortnight out than on Sunday night.
  • Willingness to be on the keyboard. You will not learn this by watching, and the schedule does not really give you the option.
  • Willingness to take the Sceptic seat, which for many people turns out to be the harder half of the pairing.

What is not expected

You do not need to be able to code. You do not need statistics beyond what you already carry. You do not need to have used an AI assistant before, and several people each cohort have not.

What you do need is a willingness to be wrong in front of other people. That is a different sort of demand from a technical one, and for a room of senior people it is usually the real one. It is also, in our experience, much easier here than it sounds now — everybody in the room has agreed to the same terms, and the week is arranged so that being wrong early is the cheapest thing you can do.

On the origins of this format

The structure of these materials — a page per session, a hex badge in the corner, all of it published openly rather than locked inside a learning platform — comes from the open teaching movement in academic statistics, and particularly from university data-skills teams who concluded that giving their courses away made those courses better. Publishing forces a kind of honesty into teaching material that private slides never quite acquire.

The pedagogy has been rebuilt for a very different room: people with profit-and-loss responsibility, an assistant that writes code faster than they can read it, and a Friday afternoon panel that does not care how elegant the model is.

What survives unchanged from the original is the conviction that materials should be free, complete and permanent. This site stays up after the cohort ends, and every version of it is public.
