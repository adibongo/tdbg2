Glossary

Every term used in the programme, defined for a business audience

This page is written for somebody who has to use a term in a meeting on Thursday, not pass an exam on it in June. Each entry starts with the idea in plain language, then attaches the proper name to it, then says why it matters commercially and where in the week you will meet it. That order is deliberate: a term you understand but cannot name is hard to look up afterwards, and a term you can name but do not understand is worse than useless, because it gives an argument the sound of authority without the substance.

Where a term is routinely misused — and several of the most important ones are — the misuse is named alongside the definition, because recognising the wrong version in somebody else’s report is at least half of the value.

You are not expected to read this page in one sitting. It is here to be consulted during the week and afterwards, and the twelve terms that matter most are also printed on a single page of the participant guide.

A

A/B test — An experiment in which the units you care about, whether customers, sessions or branches, are assigned at random to a control condition and one or more treatments, and you then compare what happened. The randomisation is doing all the work here: it is what makes the groups comparable on the hundreds of things you never thought to measure, which is why an A/B test can support a causal claim that no amount of clever observational analysis can. Everything else about the test — the statistics, the dashboard, the significance threshold — is secondary to whether the assignment was genuinely random and stayed that way. You will design and interrogate one on Friday morning in 5.2.

AUC — Area under the ROC curve: a single number between 0 and 1 summarising how well a model ranks positive cases above negative ones. 0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect. It is a genuinely useful diagnostic for the person building the model and a poor way to talk to a board, because nobody can convert it into money — which is why the programme asks you to report lift instead. Treat an unexpectedly high AUC as a bug report until you have proved otherwise; on Wednesday afternoon you will meet a model that scores beautifully for reasons you would not want to explain to a regulator.

Analytics engineer — The person who builds the tables everybody else trusts: the pipelines, the data models and the tests that sit between a raw system extract and something an analyst can reason about. It is a relatively new job title for work that has always existed, and it is usually the right first hire for an organisation whose analysts spend most of their week cleaning rather than analysing. Organisations more often hire a data scientist first, then discover that a well-paid modeller is doing plumbing. The choice comes up on Friday afternoon in 5.5.

B

Backtest — Testing a forecast against periods the model has never seen, repeatedly, by rolling the training cut-off forward through history and re-forecasting from each position. It is the only forecast evidence that means anything, because a model that has already seen the answer will reproduce it: in-sample fit measures memory rather than skill. Practically, a backtest is the difference between “our model fits the last three years well” and “our model would have been useful in each of the last three years”, and only the second is a claim a planner can act on. See 3.3.

Bimodal — A distribution with two distinct humps rather than a single central lump. Almost always it means two populations have been mixed together and handed to you as one: two products, two channels, two customer types, two periods, or the same measure taken before and after a definition changed. The commercial significance is that every summary statistic computed on a bimodal distribution describes a typical case that does not exist, which is why Wednesday morning’s 3.1 asks you to plot the shape before you compute the average.

C

Cardinality — How many rows on one side of a relationship match a row on the other: one-to-one, many-to-one, one-to-many. The phrase to hold on to is that a join is a claim about cardinality — when you join two tables you are asserting, usually without noticing, that each row on one side meets exactly one row on the other. A large share of analytical accidents are that unstated claim turning out to be false, and Tuesday afternoon’s 2.4 is built around watching one happen.

Causal claim — The assertion that changing X changes Y, as distinct from the observation that X and Y move together. Supporting one properly requires an experiment or a carefully argued quasi-experimental design, and the gap between what most business analysis can support and what most business language claims is wide. Watch the verbs, in your own writing as much as anyone else’s: drives, causes, improves, boosts and leads to are all causal claims wearing ordinary clothes. See 5.1.

Confounder — Something that causes both the thing you changed and the thing you measured, so that the two look related when neither is moving the other. Seasonal demand is the classic commercial example: it lifts marketing spend and conversions at the same time, and a naive comparison then credits the campaign with the season. Confounders you have thought of can sometimes be adjusted for; the reason randomisation is worth so much is that it handles the ones you have not.

Confidence interval — A range that would contain the true underlying parameter a stated proportion of the time if you repeated the whole procedure many times. It is a statement about a quantity you are estimating — a conversion rate, an average basket, a treatment effect — rather than about any single future observation.

Either way — see prediction interval — an 80% interval promises to be wrong one time in five, on purpose. That is not a defect to be engineered away. An interval that is never wrong is simply too wide to inform a decision, and the daily one-number exercise is designed to make that trade-off something you feel rather than something you are told.

Contribution (SHAP value) — A precise answer to the question “how much did this feature move this particular prediction away from the model’s baseline?”, derived from cooperative game theory. It is genuinely useful and genuinely well defined, and it is not a causal driver: a SHAP chart tells you how the model is reasoning, not how the world works. The practical test is that a “top drivers” chart which reorders itself when you swap the model algorithm is describing your model, and boardrooms routinely read it as describing the business. Friday morning is largely about that distinction.

Correlation — X and Y move together, and knowing one tells you something useful about the other. That is sufficient for prediction, targeting and segmentation, all of which are valuable, and it is insufficient for deciding to change X in the hope that Y follows. Much of the commercial value of Friday morning lies in being able to say which of the three claims — correlation, contribution or cause — your evidence actually supports, out loud, before somebody else says it for you.

CTE — Common table expression: the WITH step AS (...) construction in SQL, which lets a query be written as a sequence of named steps rather than as subqueries nested inside one another. The difference is not cosmetic. A query written as named steps can be read aloud, audited one stage at a time, and stopped halfway to check a row count; a deeply nested query can only be trusted or distrusted whole. Since an assistant will happily write either, asking for CTEs is one of the cheapest ways to keep generated code reviewable. See 2.1.

D

Data contract — A written set of conditions that data must satisfy, expressed as business rules that cannot be false — receipts follow orders, percentages sum to 100, a customer cannot churn twice in the same month — checked automatically, with a pass or fail result rather than a discussion. The shift it represents is from data quality as an occasional clean-up project to data quality as something a machine asserts every time the pipeline runs. It is also the point at which “please check the data” becomes an instruction somebody can actually carry out. See 2.5.

Dimension — A table of things that exist: customers, products, depots, suppliers, dates. Dimensions tend to be short and wide, carrying many descriptive attributes for relatively few rows, and they should have exactly one row per thing. That last property is an assumption rather than a guarantee, and it is worth verifying on any dimension table before you join it to anything, because a dimension with an unnoticed extra row will quietly change every total downstream of it.

Difference-in-differences — Comparing the change in a treated group with the change in an untreated group over the same period, so that anything affecting both groups equally cancels out. It is the workhorse of causal analysis when you cannot randomise, and it rests entirely on parallel trends: the assumption that, absent the intervention, the two groups would have moved together. That assumption is not testable directly, but the pre-period is, so plot it and show it. See 5.3.

Drift — What happens when the world moves away from the data a model was trained on. Feature drift means the inputs change; score drift means the outputs change; concept drift means the relationship between them changes, which is the dangerous one because the model carries on producing confident, plausible, wrong numbers. The commercial answer is unglamorous: monitor monthly, agree in advance what a drift signal triggers, and give the model a retirement date. Governance on Friday treats all three as operational rather than theoretical concerns.

F

Fact table — A table of things that happen: sales, orders, calls, payments, shipments. Facts tend to be long and thin — many rows, few columns — carrying dates, quantities and keys that point into the dimensions. Most of the numbers your organisation reports are sums over a fact table, which is why knowing its grain is the precondition for trusting any of them.

Fan-out — What happens when a join multiplies rows because the “one” side of a many-to-one relationship turns out to have more than one matching row, so that every row on the other side is duplicated. It is the most common source of silently wrong totals in commercial analysis, and it is silent precisely because nothing errors: the query runs, the chart draws, and the number is simply larger than it should be. The defence is a habit rather than a technique — count the rows before every join and after it, and expect the answer in advance. Tuesday afternoon is built on it.

G

GAID 2025 — The Nigeria Data Protection Commission’s General Application and Implementation Directive, issued under the NDPA on 20 March 2025 and effective from 19 September 2025. Where the Act sets the principles, the Directive supplies the operational detail an organisation actually has to comply with: registration, audit filing, data protection officer requirements, and the instruments permitting cross-border transfer — which matters more than it sounds when the assistant processing your customer data runs on somebody else’s infrastructure abroad. See 5.4.

Grain — What one row of a table represents, stated in a sentence: “one row per depot per SKU per week.” If you cannot say it, you cannot trust any total computed from the table, because you do not know what you are adding up. It is the single most useful question a non-technical executive can ask in a data conversation, it requires no technical knowledge whatsoever to ask, and it is the first of the Sceptic’s seven standing questions for exactly that reason.

H

Holdout — Data, or people, deliberately withheld from a model or an intervention so that performance can be measured against something rather than asserted. A retention-call holdout — 10% of the target list, deliberately not called — feels like leaving money on the table and is the only way to learn whether the calls are worth making at all. The general principle is that an intervention with no untreated comparison group cannot be evaluated, only defended, and the cost of the holdout is almost always smaller than the cost of running an ineffective programme for another two years.

I

Idempotent — A property of a process: running it twice produces the same result as running it once. A pipeline that is not idempotent — one that appends rather than replaces, or that depends on what happened to be in a folder that morning — will eventually give two different answers to the same question, and nobody will be able to say which is right. This is why Tuesday’s lab asks you to run your pipeline twice and prove the answers match, which sounds trivial until the first time it does not.

L

Leakage — A feature that encodes information which would not have been available at the moment you would actually have acted. It is the most dangerous failure in applied modelling because it announces itself as success rather than as an error: the model scores brilliantly, everyone is pleased, and the review that might have caught it is skipped because the numbers look so good. The test is a single question about time — would I have known this in time? — and it needs no mathematics at all. See 3.5.

Lift — How much better a model’s top-scored group is than a random group of the same size. It is the honest translation of model performance into something a commercial audience can act on, because it maps directly onto a campaign: three times the base rate in the top decile is a sentence a board understands and can budget against, whereas an AUC of 0.77 is a sentence that ends the conversation. Report lift, keep AUC for the model review.

M

MASE — Mean absolute scaled error: your forecast’s error divided by the error a one-step seasonal-naive rule makes in the training data. That in-sample denominator is the part most people get wrong, and it matters, because it is what makes the measure comparable across series of wildly different sizes. Below 1 means your out-of-sample error beats the naive rule’s in-sample error, which is a demanding bar and not quite the same claim as “we beat naive on the holdout”. It is preferred here to MAPE, which is undefined at zero, explodes near zero, and penalises over- and under-forecasting asymmetrically — three properties that make it actively misleading on the intermittent demand series most businesses actually have.

Model card — One page per model, written in plain language: what it is for, what it is not for, the training data, the features, what was excluded and why, performance overall and on the worst-performing segment, known limitations, where a human reviews it, how it is monitored, and the date it retires. The two lines nobody fills in are “Not for” and “Retire by”, which are also the two that would have prevented most of the model failures anybody can name. You will write one on Friday and leave with it.

N

NDPA 2023 — The Nigeria Data Protection Act, the statute governing personal data processing in Nigeria. In outline: you need a lawful basis before you process; a breach must be notified to the Commission within 72 hours of discovery, with a higher threshold before affected data subjects must be told; a DPIA is required where processing is likely to be high risk, with prior consultation if the DPIA confirms it; cross-border transfers are conditional; data subjects have rights including protection against solely automated decisions; and penalties reach ₦10m or 2% of gross revenue for data controllers of major importance. Friday’s governance session works through what each of those means for an analytics function rather than for a legal department.

DPIA — Data protection impact assessment: a structured, written assessment of the risk a piece of processing creates for the people in the data, required under NDPA s.28 wherever processing is likely to result in a high risk to rights and freedoms. Systematic profiling of individuals usually is. The distinction worth carrying away is that a model card is good practice you adopt because it makes you better at your job, whereas a DPIA may simply be the law.

Nominal vs real — Nominal is the number as recorded; real is the same number adjusted for inflation so that periods can be compared. In a high-inflation economy this is not a technicality, because every nominal series is a currency story wearing a growth story’s clothes, and a business can report handsome growth while shipping fewer units than last year. Ask “real or nominal?” first and “deflated by what?” second — the fourth of the Sceptic’s seven questions, and the one that most often changes a headline.

P

Parquet — A columnar file format that stores column types inside the file itself. It is smaller and faster than CSV, and — more importantly for anyone who has lost a morning to it — nothing downstream has to guess whether your account numbers are integers and helpfully strip the leading zeros. Tuesday’s session on the laptop warehouse uses it as the format you move data around in once it has left the spreadsheet.

Placebo test — Re-running a causal analysis at a date when nothing happened, or on units that were never treated, to see whether your method finds an effect anyway. It should find nothing. If it does find something, your design is picking up a pre-existing divergence rather than the intervention, and you have learned that before presenting rather than afterwards. It is four lines of code and disproportionately persuasive in front of a sceptical audience, which is a rare combination.

Prediction interval — A range expected to contain a future observation, as opposed to a parameter. It is always wider than a confidence interval computed on the same data, because it has to carry both the uncertainty in the estimate and the natural variability of the thing itself. Every “80% interval” in this programme’s forecasting sessions is a prediction interval. Confusing the two is not an academic error: it is how a planner ends up setting a safety-stock level from a range that describes the average week rather than next week.

Power — The probability that a test detects an effect of a given size, assuming that effect is real. It is computed before the experiment starts, because its purpose is to tell you how large a sample and how long a run you need for the result to be worth having. An underpowered test that finds nothing has not told you the treatment does not work; it has told you nothing at all, at the cost of a quarter.

Propensity model — A model that scores the probability of an event: this customer churning, this invoice going unpaid, this lead converting. The point the programme keeps returning to is that probability is only one of three things you need before you act. The others are the value at stake if it happens and whether your intervention actually changes the outcome — and a targeting list ranked on probability alone will faithfully send your best retention agents to the customers who were always going to leave.

R

RFM — Recency, frequency, monetary: a segmentation approach roughly fifty years old that takes one query, needs no machine learning, and is understood immediately by the marketer who has to act on it. It earns its place here as the benchmark a clustered segmentation has to beat before it is worth the complexity — and on Wednesday afternoon you will find that benchmarks of this kind are considerably harder to beat than they look.

Right-skewed — A distribution in which most values are small and a few are very large. Revenue, order size, account balance, tenure and lead time all behave this way, which means this is the normal condition of commercial data rather than an exception. The consequence is practical: the mean sits above the median and misdescribes the typical case, so a plan built on an average lead time or an average basket is quietly planning for a customer who does not exist.

S

Salted dataset — A dataset containing a deliberate defect, planted so that participants get burned in a classroom rather than in a board paper. The data room carries thirteen such defects, spread unevenly across the files, with several files left deliberately clean — which is the detail that makes the exercise work, because a room told everything is dirty will hunt until it finds something whether or not anything is there. Which file holds which defect is not published before Friday afternoon.

Sample ratio mismatch (SRM) — When the arms of an experiment are not the sizes the randomisation should have produced — a 50/50 split that arrives as 52/48 on a large sample, for instance. It indicates that the assignment mechanism is broken somewhere, and the uncomfortable consequence is that it invalidates the result and cannot be adjusted away: if the groups were not formed as designed, you no longer know what they are comparable on. It takes thirty seconds to check and belongs before you look at the result, every time, because checking afterwards is checking with a motive.

Star schema — One fact table surrounded by the dimension tables it points at, arranged so the diagram looks like a star. It makes semantic models fast, and — more importantly for a commercial audience — it makes measures consistent, because “revenue” is defined once at the centre rather than reinvented in each report. Monday afternoon’s 1.5 introduces it without the jargon.

Structural break — A permanent shift in the level of a time series: a new competitor, a price change, a policy, a lost customer. A forecasting model fitted straight across one will produce persistent error in a single direction, and an interval far too wide, because it is trying to average two different worlds. It is found by plotting the series and looking, which is a recurring theme of Wednesday: several of the week’s most expensive mistakes are visible to the naked eye and invisible to a summary table.

Survivorship bias — Analysing a population that is defined by having already succeeded, and drawing conclusions about how to succeed. “More sales touchpoints win more deals” is the standard commercial example, when touchpoints are only recorded for opportunities that were already qualified. The question that catches it is the sixth of the seven: what had to be true for a row to exist in this file?

Synthetic control — A weighted combination of untreated units, built so that it tracks the treated unit closely in the period before the intervention, and then used as the counterfactual afterwards. It is the tool for the awkward case where you have one treated unit — one market, one depot, one country — and no natural comparison. Placebo tests are how the inference is made credible, since there is no conventional standard error to lean on. See 5.3.

W

Window function — SQL that computes something per group without collapsing the rows away, written as OVER (PARTITION BY ...). It is how you get shares of total, ranks, running totals, moving averages and period-on-period changes — which, between them, are most of what a commercial dashboard actually displays. It is also the piece of SQL an assistant writes well and most people never learn to write themselves, which makes it a good example of the week’s broader argument: you need to be able to read it and check it, not to produce it from memory.

The seven questions, in one place

  1. What is the grain of this table?
  2. How many rows before the join, and how many after?
  3. What period am I comparing to what period, and are they the same length?
  4. Real or nominal — and if real, deflated by what?
  5. Would I have known this at the moment I needed to act?
  6. What had to be true for a row to exist in this file?
  7. If this result is this good, what is wrong with it?

None of them requires you to write code, which is the point: they are questions about meaning rather than about syntax, and they travel back to your own desk unchanged. Together they catch most of what goes wrong in commercial analytics, and you will be asked them, and asked to ask them, every day of the week.
