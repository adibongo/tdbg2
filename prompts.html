Prompt patterns

The ones that earn their place in analytical work

A great deal has been written about prompting, and a good deal of it is folklore that stopped being true two model generations ago. Magic words, elaborate personas and threats of consequences all had their moment; none of them survives contact with a current assistant on a real analytical task.

What has held up is less exciting and considerably more useful. An assistant’s value on an analytical task is roughly proportional to how much of the surrounding business context you were willing to type. The model is fluent in SQL, R and statistics; what it cannot know is your grain, your fiscal calendar, your definitions, or which of your columns is populated only after the event you are trying to predict. Nearly every prompt on this page is a different way of supplying one of those.

What separates a prompt that works from one that does not

Before the examples, it is worth naming the four properties they share, because the patterns are far less useful than the principles behind them. Once you can see what a prompt is doing, you can write your own for a situation nobody anticipated — which is the actual goal.

They supply structure rather than volume. A short, well-chosen description of the shape of your data beats pasting the data itself, or three emails about it. Assistants reason well about schemas and grain; they reason poorly about a wall of unexplained context, in much the same way a new analyst would.

They ask for a check as well as an answer. A request for an answer produces an answer, always, whether or not one is warranted. A request that also asks how the answer could be wrong produces something you can audit, and audit is the whole point.

They constrain the output. “Return one of these categories, plus a confidence field, and do not invent new categories” is a fundamentally different instruction from “classify these”, because it makes the result countable, comparable and testable against hand-labelled truth.

They put the assistant on the other side of the argument. The single most valuable move available to you is asking it to attack a result rather than produce one. It is markedly better at critique than at self-assessment, and critique is the mode in which it is least likely to agree with you out of politeness.

The patterns below are the ones used across the programme — four foundations, then one per session — and each earns its place by changing the output rather than by sounding clever.

The four foundations

1 · Give it the shape, not the data

Three lines of description that remove most hallucinated column names before they happen.

The table is fmcg_sell_out.csv. 55,474 rows, of which 55,260 are distinct on (week_starting, depot_code, sku) — the grain is intended to be one row per depot per SKU per week and there are 214 rows too many. Jan 2024 to Mar 2026. Columns: week_starting (date), depot_code (text), sku (text), units_sold (number, nullable — blank means a stockout, not zero), unit_price_ngn (number), on_promotion (0/1), discount_pct (number), stockout_flag (0/1), revenue_ngn (number, nullable).

Write DuckDB SQL to …

Why it works. Given no schema, the assistant has to infer a plausible one, and it will — confidently, and in a house style that looks entirely reasonable — then write code against columns you do not have. Given the schema, it writes code that runs the first time. This is the cheapest improvement available anywhere on this page, and it takes about ninety seconds to prepare once per table.

The two lines people leave out are the two that do the most work.

The grain sentence lets the assistant reason about whether an aggregation is safe. And note what this version does that the obvious version does not: it says the grain is intended, and that the file does not meet it. Writing “one row per depot per SKU per week” when the file has 214 duplicates would be handing the assistant your own wrong assumption and asking it to build on it — which is precisely the failure this programme exists to prevent, committed in the prompt.

The nullability semantics — “blank means a stockout, not zero” — decides whether every average it writes is right or wrong.

2 · Ask for the check, not just the answer

Write the query. Then write a second query that would detect if the first one is wrong — specifically checking the grain, unexpected row-count changes across joins, and whether any aggregation is silently dropping nulls.

Why it works. Assistants are markedly better at auditing a specific artefact than at auditing an open situation, in the same way that a colleague finds it easier to review a draft than to imagine one. Giving it something concrete to attack — a query it has just written — reliably surfaces problems that “is this right?” never will. The second query is also worth keeping: it becomes the first rule in your data contract on Tuesday afternoon.

3 · Make it argue against itself

Give me the three strongest reasons this result might be an artefact of the data rather than a fact about the business.

Why it works. “Is this right?” tends to return yes, because agreement is the conversational default and the model has no independent stake in the answer. Asking instead for the strongest available counter-argument returns a different class of response entirely: it reframes the task from assistance to critique, and critique is where an assistant is most useful to somebody who already knows the business. Read the three reasons it gives you, and notice which one you were hoping it would not mention.

4 · Refuse the first answer once, on principle

That is plausible. Now assume it is wrong. What would have to be true?

Why it works. Rather less because of what the assistant produces and rather more because of what the habit does to you. The first answer to any question arrives fluent, formatted and plausible, and the temptation to accept it is strongest precisely when you are short of time. Refusing it once, as a matter of routine rather than suspicion, installs a pause where one would not otherwise exist — and the pause is where most of the errors in this programme get caught.

By session

The patterns below are used on the days indicated, and each links to the session where it is introduced and worked through properly. They are written out here in full so that you can copy them, adapt the bracketed parts to your own situation, and take them back to your desk without having to remember what was on the screen.

Framing a use case — 1.1

I am going to describe an analytics initiative. Do not tell me it is a good idea. Instead: (1) classify it as decision-improving, cycle-time, new-revenue or visibility; (2) name the specific recurring decision it changes, or say clearly that it does not change one; (3) name whose existing judgement it displaces; (4) give me the three assumptions that, if wrong, make it worthless. Be blunt.

The initiative is: …

The instruction not to evaluate the idea is the important part. Asked whether an initiative is a good one, an assistant will tell you it is, and the answer will read well. Asked to classify it and to name what it rests on, it becomes a useful thinking partner instead of an enthusiastic one.

Stressing a value case — 1.3

Here is a value hypothesis. Do not improve it. Instead: (1) identify every number that is an assumption rather than a measurement; (2) for each, say what evidence would replace it and roughly what that evidence costs to obtain; (3) tell me which single assumption, if wrong by a factor of two, changes the decision; (4) give me the strongest argument a sceptical CFO would make against the whole case.

Point (3) is the one that earns its keep. Most value cases have a single assumption carrying the entire argument, and finding out which one before the CFO does is worth the ten minutes this takes.

Auditable SQL — 2.1

Rewrite this query as a sequence of named CTEs, one per logical step, with a comment on each saying what it produces and what its grain is. Do not change the result.

“Do not change the result” matters: without it you will get a tidier query that also quietly answers a slightly different question. With it, you get the same answer in a form you can read aloud to somebody who has never seen SQL — which is the form in which errors become visible.

Data quality rules — 2.5

Here are the columns and types, and here is what one row represents. Propose twelve data quality rules as business rules that cannot be false — not type checks. For each: the rule in English, the SQL that counts violations, and a severity of fatal, warn or note.

Asking for rules as business statements rather than type checks is what makes the output worth reading. A type check tells you a column contains numbers; a business rule tells you that a receipt cannot precede the order it belongs to, and that is the sort of rule whose violation means something has genuinely gone wrong upstream.

Exploratory analysis — 3.1

What in this dataset would surprise a domain expert? Give me five specific things to check, as queries. Do not summarise the data — I want the checks.

Refusing the summary is deliberate. A summary of a dataset is comforting and rarely informative; a list of five specific things that would surprise somebody who knows the business is a morning’s work, and you can run each of them yourself in a few seconds.

Charts — 3.2

Here is my data and the single claim I want the chart to make: “[claim]”. Propose three chart forms that would support that claim, say what each one hides, and recommend one. Then write the code with the claim as the title and direct labels rather than a legend.

And afterwards:

Look at this chart specification. What conclusion could a reader draw from it that the underlying data does not support?

Forecasting — 3.3

Forecast this weekly series 13 weeks ahead. Requirements: fit NAIVE and SNAIVE benchmarks alongside ETS and ARIMA; evaluate with rolling-origin cross-validation on a 13-week horizon and report MASE; check for structural breaks and tell me if you find one; produce 80% intervals; and state in one sentence what the interval assumes.

Segmentation — 3.4

Cluster this data. Requirements: log-transform the skewed monetary variables, standardise everything, fit k from 2 to 9, report both the within-cluster sum of squares and a profile table of segment medians for each k. Do not choose k for me and do not name the segments — I will do both. Then run a split-half stability check.

Withholding the choice of k, and the naming of the segments, is not an act of self-denial. Choosing how many segments to run and what to call them is a commercial judgement about what your organisation can actually operate, and it is the one part of a segmentation that an assistant has no basis whatsoever for making.

Leakage detection — 3.5

Here are the columns in my training data, a one-line description of what each means, and when each is populated in the business process. Identify any feature that could only be known after the outcome occurred, or that is a proxy for a protected characteristic. Explain your reasoning for each flag.

The clause in bold is doing all the work here. Leakage is a question about when a value becomes known, and that information exists nowhere in the data itself — no amount of inspection will reveal that a field is populated by the retention team after the customer has already given notice. Supply the timing and the assistant is genuinely good at this; withhold it and there is nothing it can do but guess.

Classification taxonomy — 4.2

Classify each review into exactly one of these categories: [list]. Include other and not_applicable. Also return a confidence field from 1 to 5. Return only structured output matching this schema. Do not invent categories.

Forcing an other and a not_applicable category, and refusing new ones, is what turns a classification into a measurement. Without those two escape hatches the model will squeeze awkward rows into whichever category fits least badly, and you will never see it happen.

Dashboard scope — 4.3

I need a dashboard for exactly one decision: [decision]. The user is [role], at [moment], with [time available], choosing between [actions]. Give me the minimum set of numbers that discriminates between those actions — no more than four — and tell me what you deliberately left out and why.

The closing instruction is the useful half. What an assistant deliberately left out, and why, is a much better conversation than what it chose to include — and it is the conversation you will otherwise have with the dashboard’s first user, in front of an audience.

Reproducible documents — 4.4

Every number that appears in prose must be inline code that computes it from the data. Do not hard-code any figure anywhere in the document text.

This one belongs permanently in your system prompt or project instructions rather than being retyped each time. Left to themselves, assistants write numbers directly into prose as literal text — confidently, correctly at the moment of writing, and permanently thereafter. The document then looks reproducible while quietly carrying last month’s figures in its sentences, which is a worse position than not having automated it at all.

The hostile reviewer — 4.5

Here is a two-page analytical memo. Act as the CFO it is addressed to. You are sceptical, short of time, and you have been given optimistic analytics numbers before. Give me the five questions you would ask, in the order you would ask them, and tell me which one you think the author is least prepared for.

Asking which question the author is least prepared for is what makes this more than a checklist. It is also, in most cohorts, uncomfortably accurate.

Auditing your own verbs — 5.1

For each sentence in this paragraph, classify the claim as correlational, contributional or causal. For every causal claim, state what study design would be required to support it, and rewrite the sentence to match the evidence I actually have, which is: [describe your data].

Run your capstone memo through this the night before the defence. It takes a few minutes, and it usually finds one sentence whose verb is claiming rather more than the evidence behind it — which is the sentence the panel would otherwise have found for you.

Causal design — 5.3

Estimate the effect of an intervention on [date] on [group] using difference-in-differences against [comparison]. Requirements: first plot and assess pre-period parallel trends and tell me honestly whether the design is valid; then estimate with a confidence interval; then run a placebo test at a date thirteen weeks earlier; then list three things that could have changed at the same time as the intervention and how I would check each.

Notice the order of the requirements: assess the design first, and only then estimate. A prompt that asks for the estimate first will get one, and the assumption it depends on will be mentioned politely somewhere in the third paragraph, after you have already written the number down.

Three things that do not work

These are worth listing because all three are widely recommended, and two of them were once good advice. If you are doing any of them, you are in respectable company and can stop without embarrassment.

“You are a world-class expert data scientist.” Role-play preambles genuinely helped with earlier models, which is why they are still passed around. On a current assistant they add tokens and very little else. Describing the task and the audience precisely does the work the persona was standing in for, and does it better, because it says something true about your situation rather than something flattering about the model’s.

Long unstructured context dumps. Pasting an entire messy schema, three email threads and a policy document tends to produce a worse answer than three well-chosen lines would have. Relevance beats volume: the useful information gets diluted, and the assistant has no way of knowing which paragraph you actually cared about.

Asking an open “how confident are you?” about a free-form answer. What comes back is a number that sounds like a probability and has nothing behind it, and — more to the point — nothing you could check even if you wanted to. It gives the exchange a reassuring quality that the underlying answer has not earned.

That is different from asking for a confidence field on a constrained classification, which is the pattern used in 4.2 and which works: on a fixed taxonomy with a validation set, self-reported confidence is imperfectly but usefully correlated with correctness, so routing the low-confidence rows to a human is a real saving. The difference is that in that case you have measured the calibration — which is the whole point of the 200-row validation set.

Rule of thumb: ask for confidence where you can check it, and ask what evidence would change the answer where you cannot.

The one that matters most

None of these patterns gives an assistant the ability to know things about your business that nobody has told it, and it is worth being blunt about that because the fluency of the output suggests otherwise.

It does not know that your fiscal year starts in April, that a negative quantity is a legitimate return in your ERP rather than a data error, that account numbers beginning 07 are dormant by convention, or that the Head of Supply Chain has history with the Kaduna depot and will read any analysis of it as an attack.

Each of those has cost somebody in a previous cohort an afternoon, and in every case the work was competent and the answer was wrong for a reason no amount of prompt engineering would have reached. The fix is not a cleverer prompt. The fix is you, spending two minutes typing the context that only you have.
