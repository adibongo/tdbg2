The operating model

From pilot to portfolio — Session 5.5, 30 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can choose an operating model for analytics in your organisation, know which role to hire first, and name the three mechanisms that separate organisations with a portfolio from organisations with a graveyard of pilots.

What an operating model actually is

“Operating model” is one of those phrases that can mean almost anything, so it is worth pinning down before choosing between versions of it. For analytics, it is the set of standing answers to six questions — standing, in the sense that they are decided once and then apply to every piece of work, rather than being renegotiated each time somebody asks for a number.

  1. Where do the analysts sit? In one central team, in the business units, or in some arrangement of both.
  2. Who owns a definition? When “revenue” or “active customer” or “availability” needs a precise meaning, who decides, and what happens to somebody who wants a different one.
  3. How does work get chosen? What the queue is, who sets its order, and how something urgent overtakes something important.
  4. How does something get into production? What has to be true before a number, a table or a model is allowed to be relied upon by other people — tests, review, documentation, an owner.
  5. Who is accountable for the output being used? Not for producing it. For it changing a decision.
  6. How is value checked afterwards? What is recorded when something ships, and who looks back.

Most organisations have explicit answers to the first and third of those, vague answers to the second and fourth, and no answer at all to the fifth and sixth. That distribution is the reason analytics functions can look busy, well-staffed and productive while the business quietly struggles to name a decision that changed. The rest of this session is about the questions in the second half of the list, because the first half is where attention naturally goes and the second half is where the value is.

Three shapes, and the honest trade

The first question — where the analysts sit — has three standard answers. None of them is right in general; each buys something and pays for it somewhere else, and the table is an attempt to be honest about the payment.

Centralised Federated Hub and spoke
Analysts sit In one team In the business units Both
Standards Strong Weak Set centrally, applied locally
Business context Weak Strong Adequate, if rotation happens
Speed Slow — queue Fast Fast for the spokes
Duplication Low High Medium
Fails by Becoming a ticket queue nobody uses Producing five incompatible versions of “revenue” Hub becoming a policeman

Read the last row first, because how a model fails tells you more than how it is meant to work.

A centralised function fails by becoming a ticket queue. The standards are excellent, the code is clean, and the turnaround is six weeks — so the commercial teams stop asking, build their own spreadsheets, and the central team ends up producing high-quality analysis of questions nobody is waiting for. Nobody decided this; it is what happens when the cost of asking exceeds the value of waiting.

A federated function fails by fragmentation. Every unit moves quickly and produces its own numbers, and then one Thursday three of them present revenue figures for the same quarter that do not agree, and the meeting spends its time on reconciliation instead of on the decision. The analysts were all competent. They were answering slightly different questions with slightly different filters, and no mechanism existed to notice.

Hub and spoke fails by the hub becoming a policeman: a body that reviews, approves and says no, without producing anything the spokes want. Once that happens the spokes route around it, and you have a federated model with an extra approval step.

Most organisations should be hub and spoke, and most say they are while actually running one of the other two — usually without anyone intending to mislead, because the org chart genuinely shows a hub and some spokes.

The test is not the chart. It is two questions:

  1. Does the hub set the definitions, own the pipeline standards and hold the model register — or does it just take requests? A hub that takes requests is a central team with a friendlier name, and you are centralised.
  2. Do the spokes have anybody who can read a query? If the people in the business units can only consume finished outputs, they cannot check anything, cannot ask a second question without raising a ticket, and cannot be a spoke. You are centralised with extra steps.

The good version is recognisable by a specific behaviour: someone in a business unit writes their own analysis using tables and definitions the hub maintains, the hub reviews it because reviewing is genuinely useful rather than because approval is required, and neither party thinks of the other as an obstacle. When that is happening, the arrangement is working regardless of what the chart says.

Who to hire first

The instinct is to hire a data scientist, and it is understandable — it is the role with the clearest identity and the most obvious connection to the thing you want. It is usually the wrong first hire, and the failure it produces is specific enough to describe.

A capable data scientist joins a mid-sized organisation with no reliable tables. There is nothing trustworthy to model on, so they spend their first months assembling extracts by hand, discover that the extracts disagree with the finance system, spend more months reconciling, and eventually build something impressive on a dataset only they can reproduce. Eighteen months in, they leave — not because anybody treated them badly, but because they were hired to do one job and spent their time doing a different and less interesting one. The organisation concludes that data science does not work here.

The right first hire is nearly always an analytics engineer: somebody who builds pipelines, models data properly, writes tests, and produces tables that other people can rely on without checking. It is an unglamorous role, it is in short supply, and it is the thing that makes every subsequent hire productive, because everyone who comes afterwards starts from data they can trust rather than from data they have to rebuild.

Order Role Why
1 Analytics engineer Trustworthy tables. Everything else depends on this.
2 Business analyst embedded in a function Knows the decision. Reads code. Translates.
3 Data scientist Now has clean data and a real question
4 Platform / infrastructure When scale genuinely demands it, not before

The second row is the one most often skipped, and it is the one that decides whether anything gets used. A business analyst embedded in a function sits close enough to the decision to know what it actually turns on, and is technical enough to read what the hub produced and say “this does not answer the question we ask on a Tuesday”. Without that person, requirements arrive as requests for charts, and charts are what you get.

And then the role nobody budgets for and every organisation needs: somebody whose job is to make the output get used. Change management, under another name. In most organisations this is the binding constraint on value — not the modelling, not the infrastructure — and it is typically filled by an enthusiastic volunteer doing it alongside their real job until they burn out. If you can fund only one thing beyond the analytics engineer, consider funding this instead of the next technical role.

On buying versus building

The cost of building analytics capability has collapsed over the past few years. The cost of maintaining it has not moved at all, and the gap between those two facts is where a lot of plans go wrong.

Every model you deploy is a permanent commitment. It needs monitoring, because the world it learned from moves. It needs retraining, and then revalidating, which is a different and larger job than training it the first time. It needs documentation that stays current. And it needs somebody who can explain it when the person who built it has left, which they will. A reasonable planning assumption is to budget roughly as much for the second year of a model’s life as for the first — and then to ask, of every model somebody proposes, whether a rule would capture most of the value.

A rule that gets 60% of the benefit, that a depot manager can be told in one sentence, and that anybody can check by hand is frequently the better decision. It is also a decision that people who have just learned to build models find very hard to make, and there is no shame in that: it is hard to argue for the less sophisticated option immediately after acquiring the sophisticated one. Making the comparison explicit — what does the model add over the rule, and what does that increment cost to keep alive for three years — turns it from a matter of taste into a matter of arithmetic.

Three mechanisms that turn pilots into a portfolio

Here is the difference between an organisation with an analytics portfolio and one with a graveyard of pilots, and it is not talent and it is not budget. It is three mechanisms, each of which is cheap, dull, and almost never in place.

1 · A model register with a retirement date. One row per model in production: what it is, who owns it, what decision it feeds, when it was last validated, and the date by which it must be revalidated or switched off. Without it, models accumulate. Nobody removes anything, because removing something requires knowing what depends on it, and after a few years no one does. Eventually one of them fails in a way that reaches the board, and the question asked will be “who owned this?” — a question the register answers in five seconds and its absence cannot answer at all.

2 · A decision log. For every analytics-supported decision: what was decided, on what evidence, what was expected to happen, and — the line everybody omits — what actually happened. That last column is the whole point. Six months of an honest decision log is the most valuable management asset the function will ever produce, because it is the only thing that closes the loop back to 1.1 and tells you whether the analytics is working. It also transforms the annual budget conversation, which otherwise turns on anecdotes and whoever remembers the good quarter.

Keep it short enough that people fill it in. Five fields, one screen, filled in at the moment of the decision and revisited once, on a date set at the time.

3 · A quarterly portfolio review that kills things. Not a status update in which each initiative is reported as amber and continues. A review at which at least one thing is stopped, with the stopping treated as a normal outcome rather than as a failure attributable to a person. An analytics portfolio that has never stopped anything is not being managed; it is being accumulated, and its capacity is being consumed by work that nobody would start today.

The reason to name the killing explicitly is that stopping things is socially expensive. Somebody built it, somebody sponsored it, and in most organisations the path of least resistance is to let it continue at low intensity forever. Making “we stopped one” an expected output of the review is what makes it possible for an individual to propose stopping their own project without it reading as an admission.

The 90-day plan that goes in your capstone

Three milestones, in an order chosen so that the first one requires nobody’s permission — which matters more than it sounds, because a plan whose first step needs a committee is a plan that starts in month three.

The shape that gets funded

Days 1–14 · Prove the pipeline. Raw extract to tested table, running, with the quality contract. No new spend, no new headcount, no permissions. Deliverable: the pipeline runs and one number is defensible.

Days 15–45 · Prove the decision changes. One person, one recurring decision, using the output. Not a rollout — one person. Deliverable: they used it, and you can say what they did differently.

Days 46–90 · Prove the value, with a comparison. The randomised or quasi-experimental design from 5.2 or 5.3. Deliverable: a number with an interval, and the honest statement of what design produced it.

Each stage is deliberately a proof of something different, and the order is not arbitrary. It proves the plumbing before the decision, and the decision before the value, because a plan that tries to prove value first has to assume the other two and will be attacked on both.

At Day 90 you either have evidence and a case for scaling, or you have learned cheaply that the value hypothesis was wrong — which is also a success, and is the outcome you should say out loud when you present the plan rather than leaving it to be discovered. Saying it disarms the reviewer whose real objection is “what if it doesn’t work”, because you have already told them what happens in that case and how little it costs.

The thing that will actually stop you

It is worth being direct about this, because plans of the kind above are usually derailed by something that appears nowhere in them. It is not the technology, which is adequate. It is not the data, which is imperfect everywhere and has never on its own stopped anything. It is not skills, which can be bought or taught.

It is the person whose judgement your initiative displaces — the topic of 1.1 and section 6 of your charter.

The depot manager who has ordered by feel for eleven years and is usually right. The relationship manager who knows which customers to call and why. The category buyer whose supplier relationship is not in any system. Each of them is experienced, respected, and correct often enough to have a genuine case — which is exactly what makes this hard, and why treating it as resistance to be overcome tends to fail. They are not obstacles to the analysis; in most cases they are holding information the analysis does not have.

Three things work, in ascending order of how uncomfortable they are and roughly ascending order of effectiveness:

  1. Make them the author. Bring them the model as a draft rather than as a decision, and ask what it gets wrong. They will tell you, in detail, and they will be right about some of it. Their corrections improve the model, and the model becomes something they helped build rather than something being done to them. This costs two meetings and is the single highest-return move available.
  2. Show the override log. Let them override the model. Log every override with its reason, and measure afterwards which ones improved the outcome. In many organisations roughly half of overrides turn out to be improvements — which is a genuinely useful finding, because it says the model needs the human, the human needs the model, and the conversation is no longer about replacement. It also identifies precisely where the model is weak, which is information you could not have got any other way.
  3. Change what they are measured on, rather than what they are told to do. This is the one that requires seniority, and it is the one that actually settles the matter. If the depot manager is still measured on stock cover while the dashboard optimises for service level and obsolescence, then following the dashboard makes their numbers worse. No amount of training, communication or executive sponsorship will fix that, and they are entirely right to ignore it. Fix the measure and the behaviour follows without an argument.

Takeaways

  1. Hub and spoke, in practice as well as on the chart. The test is who owns definitions.
  2. Hire the analytics engineer first. Trustworthy tables come before clever models.
  3. Budget the second year as heavily as the first. Ask whether a rule would do.
  4. Model register with retirement dates. Decision log with outcomes. A quarterly review that kills things.
  5. The binding constraint is the person whose judgement you are displacing. Make them the author.
