When you cannot randomise

Difference-in-differences, synthetic control, and stating a weaker claim honestly — Session 5.3, 45 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can estimate a causal effect when randomisation was impossible, state the assumption your estimate depends on, test that assumption where you can, and say what your claim is worth in a sentence that a sceptic will accept.

Most of the time, you cannot randomise

Session 5.2 made the case for experiments, and the case is a good one. It is also, for a large share of the questions you will actually be asked, unavailable — and not because anybody was careless.

The price change went live everywhere at once, because a staggered price is a customer relations problem. The competitor entered the market on their own timetable. The regulation changed. The depot closed. The system migration happened on a Tuesday and could not have happened on half a Tuesday. Nobody was going to randomise any of those, and yet somebody will still ask what effect it had — often at the point where the answer determines a budget.

The useful news is that this situation has its own toolkit, developed largely in economics and epidemiology, where randomising is frequently impossible or unethical. The methods give you weaker claims than an experiment, they rest on assumptions you have to state out loud, and — this is the part that surprises people — being explicit about both is what makes you credible. A stated assumption invites a specific challenge, which you can then answer. An unstated one leaves the whole estimate vulnerable to a general suspicion you cannot address.

Before-and-after is not evidence

Start with what almost everybody reaches for first, because it is the most natural thing in the world to reach for:

“We changed the reorder policy in November. Availability improved. The policy worked.”

The sentence has a comforting shape and it is very hard to argue with in a meeting. But consider what else happened between November and now. The season turned. The promotion calendar moved. A supplier’s performance changed. The exchange rate moved, which changed landed cost, which changed what got ordered. A competitor repriced. And — the one people forget — a group of people started paying closer attention because they knew a change was being watched, which on its own often improves an operational metric for a while.

A before-and-after comparison attributes every one of those to your change. It does not apportion them, and it does not flag them. It quietly credits your policy with the whole of the movement. That is why it is worth being blunt about the status of this design: it is not a weak causal design that could be strengthened with more data. It is not a causal design at all, because it contains no mechanism for separating your change from everything else that happened at the same time.

Difference-in-differences

The fix is to find a comparison group that did not get the change, and the idea is exactly what its name says: compare the change in the treated group with the change in the untreated group over the same period.

The reasoning is worth walking through in words before any notation. The treated group’s before-and-after movement contains two things mixed together: the effect of the intervention, and the effect of everything else that happened over those weeks. The control group’s movement contains only the second, because they had no intervention. So if you are willing to believe that “everything else” affected both groups by the same amount, you can subtract the control group’s movement from the treated group’s, and what remains is the intervention.

An invented example makes the arithmetic concrete. Suppose the treated depots averaged 500 units a week before the change and 380 after — a fall of 120. Over the same weeks the control depots went from 400 to 440 — a rise of 40. Before-and-after would report the treated group’s fall of 120 as the effect. Difference-in-differences says the control group’s rise of 40 is what “everything else” was doing, so absent the intervention the treated group would have risen by 40 too, to 540. The estimated effect is 380 minus 540, or −160 units — larger than the naive figure, because the intervention had to overcome a favourable background trend before it could produce a fall at all. Two differences, subtracted: hence difference-in-differences, often written DiD, and you will meet it under that name in any economics paper on policy evaluation.

Note what that example also shows: the naive figure can be too small as easily as too large. It is not a conservative estimate. It is an arbitrary one.

library(tidyverse)
library(fixest)

weekly <- arrow::read_parquet("outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet")
#  no pipeline output yet?  arrow::read_parquet("outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet")
#  ships as a reference build - use it and come to the floor at the break.

# Four northern depots were re-segmented on 2025-11-03; the other eight were not.
did <- weekly |>
  mutate(treated = region %in% c("North West", "North Central"),
         post    = week_starting >= as.Date("2025-11-03")) |>
  group_by(depot_code, week_starting, treated, post) |>
  summarise(units = mean(units_sold, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

# Three decisions, and all three matter more than the arithmetic:
#   1. keep the weekly panel - do not collapse to four numbers per depot
#   2. model the LOG, because the effect is multiplicative
#   3. cluster the standard errors on the unit of treatment
feols(log(units) ~ treated:post | depot_code + week_starting,
      data = did, cluster = ~depot_code)

The treated:post term is the estimate you want: the extra movement in the treated depots after the change, over and above whatever the treated depots and that week were doing generally. The | depot_code + week_starting part handles those two “generally”s — it allows every depot its own level and every week its own level, so that a depot which is simply larger than the others, or a week in which everybody sold more, cannot be mistaken for an effect. Because the outcome is modelled in logs, the coefficient reads as a proportional change: exponentiate it and subtract one to express it as a percentage.

Three specification choices that decide whether you find anything

Here is something worth knowing before it happens to you. Run the naive version of this analysis — collapse each depot to one number per period, model units in levels rather than logs, use ordinary standard errors — and you get an estimate whose confidence interval contains zero. The honest reading of that output is that there was no detectable effect, and a careful person would report exactly that.

Run the version above and you recover −37%, with the interval nowhere near zero.

The two analyses use the same data and the same intervention date. The difference is not statistics in any deep sense; it is three modelling decisions, each of which can be argued for in advance of seeing any result.

Do not collapse the panel. Averaging each depot down to four numbers — treated or not, before or after — discards nearly all the information you collected. The week-by-week series tells you how variable a depot ordinarily is, which is the yardstick against which any change has to be judged. Throw that away and you have no yardstick, so the interval widens until it swallows everything.

Model the log if the effect is multiplicative. A re-segmentation that moves a share of demand elsewhere takes a proportion of each depot’s volume, not a fixed number of units — a big depot loses more units than a small one for the same underlying effect. Modelling in levels asks the data to find one common number of units, which fits nobody. And parallel trends in levels and parallel trends in logs are genuinely different assumptions that cannot generally both hold, so pick the one that matches how the world works, decide before you look, and say which you picked.

Cluster on the unit of treatment. Consecutive weeks within a single depot are not independent observations — they share a manager, a catchment and a stock position, so if one week is high the next is likely to be high too. Standard errors calculated as though every week were independent are therefore too small. Clustering on depot tells the calculation to treat each depot, rather than each week, as the unit that supplies genuinely new information.

Do that on this dataset, though, and you will find the clustered standard error is only about 10% wider than the unclustered one — which is worth pausing on, because the received wisdom is that clustering inflates standard errors severalfold. It does when there is strong correlation within clusters. Here the week-to-week noise inside a depot is close to independent once the depot’s own level is accounted for, so there is little for the correction to find.

The lesson is not “clustering doesn’t matter”. It is that you cluster because the design says the errors might be correlated, not because you expect a particular answer — and then you report whatever comes out. A specification chosen after seeing which one gave the more convenient interval is not a specification; it is a preference wearing one.

And then the constraint nobody can fix by being cleverer: there are four treated depots. The cluster-robust machinery assumes you have enough clusters for its approximations to hold, and four is not enough, however many weeks each of them contributes. The binding limitation on this design is the number of things that got treated, not the amount of data — which makes it a design conversation to have before the next rollout, not an analysis conversation to have after it.

The assumption, and how to check it

Everything above rests on one thing, and it is worth naming plainly before naming it technically: you are assuming that if the intervention had never happened, the two groups would have carried on moving up and down together. Not that they would sit at the same level — northern depots can be larger or smaller than the rest — but that they would move in step, so that the control group’s path is a fair stand-in for the path the treated group would have taken.

That assumption is called parallel trends, and it is the whole design. If it fails, the number you compute is not an effect; it is the divergence the two groups were going to show anyway.

You cannot test it directly, and it is worth being honest about why: it is a claim about a world that did not happen, and no data exists from a world that did not happen. What you can test is its observable implication. If the two groups really do move together absent an intervention, they should have been moving together in the weeks before the intervention, when neither had received it.

weekly |>
  mutate(grp = if_else(region %in% c("North West", "North Central"),
                       "North (treated)", "Rest (control)")) |>
  filter(week_starting < as.Date("2025-11-03")) |>
  group_by(grp, week_starting) |>
  summarise(units = mean(units_sold, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop") |>
  ggplot(aes(week_starting, units, colour = grp)) +
  geom_line() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(title = "Pre-period trends. If these diverge, the design fails.")

Plot it. Every time. The two lines will not lie on top of one another, and they are not meant to; what you are looking at is whether the slopes match and whether the wobbles happen at the same time in both. If the pre-period trends visibly diverge, difference-in-differences is simply not available to you on this data, and no amount of added controls repairs it — worth being firm about, because the instinct when the plot looks wrong is to start adding covariates until it looks better.

Putting the pre-trend plot on the page beside the estimate is the single strongest credibility move available in observational causal work. It costs four lines of code, and it shows the audience the assumption rather than asking them to take your word that it holds.

Synthetic control, when there is no obvious comparison

Sometimes only one unit was treated — one depot, one region, one market — and no single other unit is a plausible comparison for it. The largest depot has no twin; the capital city behaves like nowhere else.

The idea behind synthetic control is to build the comparison rather than to find it. Instead of picking one control unit, you take a weighted blend of several untreated units, choosing the weights so that the blend tracks the treated unit as closely as possible in the period before the intervention. If a mixture of 40% of one depot, 35% of another and 25% of a third reproduces the treated depot’s history convincingly, then that mixture is a defensible stand-in for what the treated depot would have done next. The blend becomes your counterfactual, and the gap that opens up after the intervention date is your estimate.

# Conceptually: find weights over control depots that best reproduce
# the treated depot's pre-period series, then project forward.
library(tidysynth)

# The donor pool must contain only UNTREATED units. DP04, DP09 and DP12 were
# re-segmented at the same time as DP05; leaving them in biases the estimate
# towards zero, because the "control" would have moved too.
donors <- panel_data |> filter(!depot_code %in% c("DP04", "DP09", "DP12"))

synth <- donors |>
  synthetic_control(outcome = units, unit = depot_code, time = week,
                    i_unit = "DP05", i_time = as.Date("2025-11-03")) |>
  generate_predictor(mean_units = mean(units)) |>
  generate_weights() |>
  generate_control()

synth |> plot_trends()      # treated vs synthetic
synth |> plot_differences() # the estimated effect over time
synth |> plot_placebos()    # the inference: run it on untreated units

The comment about the donor pool is the part that most often goes wrong in practice, and it goes wrong quietly. Every unit you allow into the blend must be genuinely untreated. Include one that got the same change at the same time and your “control” moves along with the treated unit, which pulls the estimated gap towards zero and makes a real effect look like nothing.

The plot_placebos() step is where the inference lives, and it is a lovely piece of reasoning. With one treated unit there is no conventional standard error to compute, so instead you run the entire procedure again pretending, in turn, that each untreated unit was the treated one. Each of those runs produces a gap you know is pure noise, because nothing happened to that unit. If your real treated unit’s gap sits comfortably among the placebo gaps, you have found noise; if it stands well outside them, you have found something. In effect you build your own null distribution out of units where you know the answer is zero.

Interrupted time series

Sometimes there is genuinely no comparison group at all — a regulation that applied to everybody on the same day, a national price change, a system that was switched over everywhere at once. You can still do better than before-and-after, by building the counterfactual out of the history of the series itself.

Fit a model to the pre-intervention period, forecast forward as though nothing had happened, and then compare what actually occurred with that forecast and its interval. The gap between the two is your estimate; the width of the forecast interval is your uncertainty, and it widens honestly as you look further past the intervention date, which is exactly as it should be.

library(fable); library(tsibble)

pre <- series |> filter(week < intervention_date)
counterfactual <- pre |> model(ARIMA(units)) |> forecast(h = "13 weeks")

# The effect is actual minus counterfactual; the uncertainty is the
# forecast interval, and it widens with the horizon - honestly.

This is 3.3 put to a causal purpose, and it inherits every caveat from that session. It is the weakest of the three designs, for a reason that is easy to state and impossible to fix: it assumes the only thing that changed at the intervention date was the intervention. In a real economy, with a promotional calendar, a currency and a competitor, that is rarely true, and the further you forecast the less true it gets.

Ranking the claims honestly

The point of this table is not to shame the lower rows: a well-executed interrupted time series presented with its assumption on the page is far more useful than a randomised experiment nobody ran. What it gives you is the sentence you are entitled to say once you have chosen a design, and the assumption you must be prepared to defend when somebody asks.

Design The claim you may make The assumption
Randomised experiment “X caused a Y% change, 95% CI [a, b]” Randomisation worked (check SRM)
Difference-in-differences “Consistent with X causing Y, assuming parallel trends, which held for N weeks before” Parallel trends, in the scale you modelled
Synthetic control “X is associated with a Y-point divergence from a matched synthetic comparison; placebo tests put it in the top k of m” The donor pool spans the treated unit
Interrupted time series “Outcomes after X were outside the 80% interval projected from history” Nothing else changed at that date
Before-and-after — This is not a causal design

The sentence that makes you credible

“This is not a randomised result. It is a difference-in-differences estimate against the eight comparison depots, in logs, with standard errors clustered on the depot. It rests on the assumption that the two groups would have moved together — and that held for the twenty-six weeks before the change, which is the plot on the left. My estimate is a 37% reduction in northern weekly volume, interval 36% to 38%. The honest limitation is that only four depots were treated, so the real interval is wider than that arithmetic suggests — cluster-robust inference with four clusters is not reliable however many weeks you have. If we want a stronger claim, the next rollout phase should be randomised, which costs nothing extra.”

Six sentences, and each is doing a specific job: refusing a claim you are not entitled to, giving the specification so somebody could reproduce it, stating the assumption and pointing at the evidence for it, giving the estimate with its interval, conceding the limitation a well-briefed sceptic would have raised — in more detail than they would have managed — and converting the whole thing into a cheap, specific next step.

Nobody in the room can attack it, and the reason is not rhetorical skill. It is that every attackable thing has already been conceded, by you, in your own words, with the remedy attached. What is left for the sceptic to do is agree with the next step, which is what you wanted anyway.

Core lab · 20 minutes

  • Plot the pre-period trends for northern versus other depots. Are they parallel? Say how you decided.
  • Run the difference-in-differences the naive way — collapsed, in levels, unclustered — then again on the weekly panel, in logs, clustered on depot. Report both intervals and explain the gap without using the word “significant”.
  • For your capstone: which design is available to you? Write the honest claim sentence for that design.
  • Sceptic: name the thing that changed at the same time as the intervention and is not accounted for. There is always one.

The second item bans the word “significant” on purpose. It does a lot of hiding, and being denied it forces you to say what actually differed between the two analyses — which is what you would need to say to a panel anyway.

Stretch

Run a placebo test on the difference-in-differences: pretend the intervention happened thirteen weeks earlier than it did, on the same groups, and re-estimate using only the data from before the real intervention date.

You should find nothing, and finding nothing is the result you want. If you find an effect at a date when, by construction, nothing happened, your design is picking up a divergence that was already under way — which means the estimate at the real date is picking up some of it too, and is not trustworthy.

Placebo tests are cheap, they take four lines, and almost nobody in a commercial setting runs them. Running one and reporting that it came back null is disproportionately persuasive, precisely because it is evidence you went looking for a reason to disbelieve your own result.

Copilot check

Assistants write difference-in-differences code correctly and quickly. What they will not do unprompted is any of the four things that decide whether the estimate means anything:

  • plot the pre-trends,
  • mention that the estimate is meaningless if the pre-trends diverge,
  • suggest a placebo test,
  • or tell you that something else changed at the same date.

This is less a defect of reasoning than of framing: you asked for an estimate, and an estimate is what you were given. The prompt that fixes it asks for the scepticism explicitly:

Estimate the effect of an intervention on [date] on [group] using difference-in-differences against [comparison]. Requirements: first plot and assess pre-period parallel trends and tell me honestly whether the design is valid; then estimate with a confidence interval; then run a placebo test at a date thirteen weeks earlier; then list three things that could have changed at the same time as the intervention and how I would check each.

The last clause is the one it cannot fully answer, and it is worth knowing that in advance rather than discovering it in front of a panel. It will generate entirely plausible candidates — seasonality, a competitor move, a systems change — with no way of knowing which of them actually happened in your business in that particular November. Supplying that is your half of the work: the division of labour from 1.4, applied to the hardest problem in the programme.

Takeaways

  1. Before-and-after is not a causal design.
  2. Difference-in-differences needs a comparison group and rests on parallel trends. Plot the pre-trends and show them.
  3. Synthetic control builds a comparison when no single unit will do. Placebo tests are the inference.
  4. Interrupted time series is the weakest option and assumes nothing else changed.
  5. State the design, the assumption, the evidence for it, the estimate, the interval, and the route to a stronger claim. Six sentences.
