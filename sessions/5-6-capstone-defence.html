Capstone · Defence

Five minutes, four minutes of questions, and somebody who does not work here — Session 5.6, 90 minutes

Aim

Defend the case you have built all week in front of a panel with no stake in being kind to you, and leave with a plan you have committed to in public. Everybody presents.

The panel, and the three rooms

Three external people, none of them faculty, none of them from this school:

  • a CFO or finance director, who will attack the value case;
  • an analytics or data leader, who will attack the method;
  • a risk, audit or compliance officer, who will attack the governance.

They are external for a reason worth naming. A faculty member who has watched you work all week knows what you meant, fills in the gaps involuntarily, and grades the effort as well as the output. Somebody who has never met you has only what you say in the room, which is exactly the condition you will be in on a Monday in six weeks’ time when you present this to your own executive committee.

From 15:30 the cohort splits into three parallel rooms, one panellist and one faculty member in each. Sixteen teams of four or five, so five or six defences per room, nine minutes each plus a minute to change over — about an hour of the ninety, with the balance deliberately left as slack. Your team gets one of the three panellists, and which one is drawn at lunchtime, so prepare for all three lines of attack rather than the one you would prefer.

That is the trade the timetable makes at this cohort size. Seventy-one individual defences would be eleven hours, so the choice was never between a team defence and an individual one; it was between a team defence and no defence at all. What a team buys back is depth: five minutes defending one case that four or five people have already argued over is a harder five minutes than five minutes defending something nobody has yet challenged.

Your individual turn was Thursday, in Lab 4.6 — ninety seconds alone, in a corner of seven. If that felt like the easier version, it was not; it was the only one where nobody could help you.

They have read your two-page brief in advance. They have not seen your slides and they do not want any.

What is being tested

Not whether your model is sophisticated. Nobody on the panel cares, and if the method is elegant they will take that as read and move on to something else.

What is being tested is whether you can stand behind a number in front of people who do not want to believe it — which is, when you strip away the tooling, the entire job this programme exists to teach.

Underneath that headline the panel is really looking for four things, and it helps to know what they are, because they are not what most people prepare for:

Do you know where your number is weakest? Every estimate rests on something that could be wrong. A panellist forms their view of you largely on whether you name that thing before they find it. Somebody who volunteers their weakest assumption is treated as a reliable narrator for the rest of the conversation; somebody who has to be led to it is not.

Can you separate what you measured from what you concluded? This is the whole of Friday morning, compressed into a habit. The panel is listening for the join between the evidence and the claim, and for whether the verb you used matches the design you ran.

Would the decision actually change? They are trying to work out whether a real person, with a name and a diary, does something different next month. A great deal of technically excellent work fails here, and it fails silently, because nobody in the room wants to ask the question that sounds naive.

Are you honest under pressure? The most informative ninety seconds of any defence is the one after a question you cannot answer. That is the part they will remember, and it is the part almost nobody rehearses.

Format

5 minutes Your team presents. No slides. You may hold your two-page brief and put one chart on the screen. Nominate whoever you like to speak.
4 minutes Questions — put to named individuals, not to the team. The panel will interrupt during the five minutes as well.
9 minutes Total, per team, plus a minute to change over. The room runs to a visible clock and it is not negotiable.

Three rooms in parallel, five or six team defences each, from 15:30. A six-team room is done by about 16:30; the slot runs to 17:00 because panels that are rushed ask worse questions.

The visible clock is not there to make the afternoon stressful. It is there because a hard stop is the only thing that reliably produces the compression this exercise is about — and because without it, one person’s overrun is taken out of somebody else’s defence, which would be unfair in a way that matters more than anybody’s comfort.

The rubric — the same one published on Monday

Dimension Weight What earns a high mark
Business impact 40% A named recurring decision, a named decision-maker, value with a range and three ranked assumptions, and an honest statement of what happens if the assumptions are wrong
Analytical rigour 30% Grain stated. Defects found and handled. Leakage checked. Uncertainty quantified. Evidence standard matched to the claim. Naive benchmark reported.
Communication 20% Number and action in the first sentence. Interval with its driving assumption. One chart that makes the case. Survives interruption.
Feasibility and governance 10% 90-day plan whose first milestone needs nobody’s permission. Lawful basis. Model card. The displaced person named.

A capstone cannot pass on rigour alone. A technically immaculate analysis of a decision nobody makes scores 30 out of 100 here, and that weighting is deliberate: it is the actual distribution of value in the world outside this room.

It is worth reading the weighting the other way round as well, because it is the more encouraging direction. A modest, well-chosen analysis of a decision somebody genuinely makes every month — clearly explained, honestly caveated, with a first step that starts on Monday — can score very well indeed on 70% of the rubric without any technical ambition at all. If your week produced less modelling than you hoped, that is not the constraint on your mark.

The five minutes

Five minutes is not eight minutes with things left out. It is a different document, and the discipline of it is the point — a senior audience gives you about this long before they have formed their view anyway.

A structure that works, and you may use it:

0:00–0:30 — The number and the action. One sentence containing a figure, a verb and a date. Then stop talking for two seconds and let it land.

0:30–1:15 — The confidence. The interval, and the single assumption that drives its width. What it would cost to narrow it.

1:15–1:45 — The ask. Specific, addressed, dated. Including “nothing, this is for information” if that is the truth.

1:45–3:00 — The evidence. Where the data came from, its grain, what you found wrong with it and what you did about it. This is your credibility, and it is Tuesday’s work.

3:00–3:45 — The evidence standard. What kind of claim you are making and what would be needed for a stronger one. Concede this before you are asked.

3:45–4:15 — Governance and the 90 days. Lawful basis, human in the loop, the first milestone.

4:15–5:00 — What happens if you are wrong, and how you would find out.

Everything else you know is in the four minutes of questions, or in the appendix nobody opens. Cutting to this shape is the last exercise of the week and it is not an accident that it is the hardest.

What a strong defence sounds like

Structures are easier to follow once you have heard one filled in, so here is the first ninety seconds of a defence built on the availability case. It is not the only good version, and your figures will be your own, but the shape of it is worth studying.

“Availability in Q1 was 96.9%, and closing the gap to 97.5% is worth ₦17.9m of gross profit a year. I am asking for approval by 30 October to change the reorder policy at three depots for one quarter, with the other nine as a comparison group, at no new spend.

My range is ₦8.1m to ₦32.5m. Almost the whole of that width is one judgement — how much stockout demand is genuinely lost rather than deferred to the following week. I have assumed a third; the range spans 15% to 60%. About two weeks of point-of-sale analysis would settle it, and I have not spent that yet because the decision does not turn on it.

The evidence is sell-out from the WMS, one row per depot per SKU per week, and I found problems in it that changed the answer. I will come to those. The claim I am making is associational, not causal: I know availability and lost sales move together, and I do not yet know that this policy closes the gap. That is exactly what the three-depot comparison is for.”

Notice what has happened in those ninety seconds. The panel has the number, the action and the date before they have formed any view of their own. They know how uncertain it is and precisely why. They have been told the data had problems, by the presenter, before anybody could find them. And the causal claim has been downgraded to what the evidence supports, with the stronger design offered as the ask — so the obvious method attack has been made by the presenter and answered in the same breath.

What that costs is nerve, not preparation. Every sentence in it is a sentence a nervous presenter would soften or leave until later.

The five questions the panel always asks

Have the answer ready. Every one of these has been the subject of a session this week.

  1. “What is the grain of this table?” — 2.4
  2. “Would you have known this at the time you needed to act?” — 3.5
  3. “Is that real or nominal?” — 1.2
  4. “How would you know if you were wrong?” — 5.2
  5. “Who loses if this works?” — 1.1

The sixth, which is not always asked but should be: “which of these numbers did the assistant produce, and which did you check?”

None of the five is a trap. Each is a question a competent panellist asks because the answer tells them how far the rest of your analysis can be trusted, and each can be answered in one sentence if you have done the work. That is why they are worth rehearsing aloud: the difference between knowing the answer and being able to say it fluently under a clock is larger than it seems from a chair.

Answering a question you cannot answer

This is the moment people fear, so it is worth planning for rather than hoping to avoid. It will happen — the panel is deliberately drawn from three different disciplines, so at least one of them will ask about something outside where you spent your week.

There are three bad responses and one good one, and the bad ones are bad in instructive ways.

Bluffing fails worst and fails permanently. Somebody on that panel does this for a living, and a confident answer with a wrong shape is immediately recognisable to them. Worse, it retrospectively contaminates everything you said before it, because the panel now has to wonder which of your other confident answers was also constructed on the spot.

Capitulating — “you’re right, that probably invalidates it” — is the opposite failure and it is more common in this room, because it feels like honesty. Usually it gives away far more than the question actually demanded. A specific objection to one assumption does not invalidate an analysis, and conceding as though it did leaves the panel unable to tell which parts of your work were sound.

Deflecting into detail — answering a question you were not asked, at length, in the hope that the clock runs out — is the most common of the three and the most transparent. The panel will simply ask again, and you will have spent a minute of your four.

The response that works has four parts, and it can be delivered in about fifteen seconds:

  1. Say plainly that you do not know. Not apologetically, and without a preamble. “I don’t know.”
  2. Say what you do know that bears on it. The adjacent fact, the related check you did run, the boundary of what you established. This is what distinguishes not knowing from not having thought about it.
  3. Say how you would find out, concretely enough that somebody could start. Which table, which comparison, roughly how long.
  4. Say what would change if the answer went either way. This is the part almost nobody does, and it is the part that turns the exchange around, because it demonstrates that you understand how much rests on the open question.

Put together: “I don’t know what the effect on returns was — I didn’t have returns data at the depot grain. What I do know is that the margin figure I used is gross of returns, so if returns rose the ₦17.9m is overstated. I would check it against the credit notes file, which is about a day’s work. If returns rose by more than about a fifth, the case stops being worth doing, and I would want that answered before the pilot rather than after.”

That answer contains an admission of ignorance and it makes the presenter sound more reliable, not less. It is the same asymmetry as in 4.5: a person who marks the edge of their knowledge precisely is easier to trust inside it.

One practical note. “I’d have to check” is fine once. Twice in nine minutes and the panel quietly reclassifies the whole item as not yet ready, however good the work behind it — which is the reason for the rehearsal below, and for writing down the questions you hope nobody asks.

The rehearsal, 14:30

Before the panel arrives you get forty-five minutes in pairs — roughly twenty minutes each way — with a Sceptic from a table you have not worked with. They have the rubric and the five questions.

Their instruction: find the question the presenter is least prepared for, and ask it twice.

Asking twice is the important half. The first answer to an uncomfortable question is usually a deflection produced under surprise; the second is the real one. Panellists know this and do it instinctively, so it is much better to meet it here.

Their second instruction: give exactly one sentence of feedback afterwards. Not a list. The one thing that, if fixed, most improves the defence.

The single-sentence limit is deliberate too. A list of eight observations delivered forty-five minutes before a defence cannot be acted on and mostly produces anxiety. One sentence can be acted on, and being forced to choose makes the Sceptic decide what actually matters.

The checklist before you stand up

  • First sentence contains a number, a verb and a date.
  • The interval is stated, and the assumption that drives its width is named.
  • You can state the grain of every table you used.
  • Every feature in your model has passed the leakage question.
  • Every number is real or nominal, and you know which.
  • Your causal verbs match your evidence. You audited them.
  • You can name the evidence standard your claim rests on, and the stronger design you would run next.
  • Model card written. Lawful basis identified. Human review defined.
  • The 90-day plan’s first milestone needs nobody’s permission.
  • You have named the person whose judgement this displaces, and what you will do about them.
  • The brief regenerates from the pipeline. You have tested this today.

If two or three of those are not true an hour before you stand up, do not attempt to fix them all. Fix the first one, which costs a single sentence and carries a fifth of the communication mark, and then be candid about the rest when they come up. An unfinished item you name yourself costs very little. The same item found by a panellist costs a great deal more.

After the defence, 17:00 — and we finish at 17:30

The failure logbook, consolidated. Every entry from every table, deduplicated into one page: the ways the copilot misled us this week. It is read aloud and sent to the cohort.

It is, reliably, the artefact people forward to their teams — and it is the honest summary of what this week was actually about. It travels because it is the one document that is useful to somebody who was not here.

The two envelopes. The number you wrote on Monday morning, with its interval. The number you wrote this morning, with its interval. Compare them, and note which changed more: the number, or the interval.

For most people it is the interval, and it will have got wider. That is worth sitting with for a moment, because the instinct is to read a wider interval as having gone backwards — five days of work and you are less sure than when you started. But the Monday interval was narrow because it was uninformed: it was drawn before you knew what the data did on the joins, before you knew which assumption the value case actually rested on, and before you had asked what evidence your verb required. The Friday interval is wider because it now includes the uncertainty that was always there and was invisible to you.

That is not a failure of the week. It is the week working. You now know more about what you do not know, which is the condition for every good decision you will make with data from here.

Certificates. Certificate of Completion this afternoon.

Certificate of Implementation at Day 90, to those who can show the number that moved — and, more importantly, how they know it moved because of what they did.
