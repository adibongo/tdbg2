Charts that survive a board room

The grammar of graphics, and the bar chart that hides its own data — Session 3.2, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can state, in one sentence, the single argument each of your charts is making; choose a chart form that supports that argument rather than merely displaying the numbers behind it; and rebuild a chart that did not land in a real meeting so that it would have.

If your Tuesday pipeline did not finish

Everything below reads outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet — the deduplicated, null-resolved, dimension-joined table that Tuesday’s lab produces.

A reference build of that exact file ships with the course data, in outputs/. If your own pipeline is not finished, point today’s code at the reference build and carry on. You will fix your pipeline at the next break or this evening; you will not spend this morning catching up while the room moves on without you.

Nobody is marked down for this. The capstone checkpoint asks whether your pipeline runs by Friday, not whether it ran by Wednesday at nine.

A chart is an argument

It is natural to think of a chart as a way of showing data — of putting the numbers on a page so that people can see them. That description is accurate and it is not very useful, because it does not help you choose between the dozen chart forms that would all show the same numbers perfectly well.

The more useful description is that a chart is an argument. It has a claim, and the visual form is the evidence offered for that claim. Once you think of it that way, the design question stops being “which chart looks best?” and becomes “which arrangement of these numbers makes the claim visible without the reader having to work?”

Most business charts that fail do not fail because they are ugly. They fail because the form does not match the claim. Somebody wanted to argue “the North is falling behind” and drew a chart that displayed “here are revenues by region” — a chart which contains the evidence, but which leaves the audience to construct the argument themselves, in their heads, during the four seconds the slide is up, while the presenter is already talking about something else. That is a great deal to ask of anybody, and the request is usually declined.

So the sequence is: write the claim as a sentence first, then choose the form. The table below is a starting point, not a rulebook — but it covers most of what a commercial pack contains.

The claim The form
X is bigger than Y Bar, sorted, one colour, difference annotated
X is changing over time Line. Never a bar
X is changing faster than Y Indexed line, both starting at 100
X is spread more widely than you think Distribution: histogram, density, box, or the points themselves
X and Y move together Scatter, with the point that breaks the pattern labelled
X is made up of these parts Stacked bar if 2–3 parts; small multiples if more
X is uncertain by this much Line with a shaded interval, or points with ranges
Most of X comes from a few Y Pareto: sorted bars with a cumulative line

Two rows deserve a note. “Never a bar” for change over time is unusually absolute for this course, and the reason is that a bar encodes a quantity by its length from zero, which implies each period is a separate thing being compared, whereas a line encodes continuity and lets the eye read the slope — which is what “changing” means. And Pareto is simply a name worth knowing: sorted bars with a cumulative percentage line running across them, so that the reader can see at a glance how much of the total the first few categories between them account for.

The title of the chart should then be the claim itself. Not “Revenue by region”, which describes the contents, but “North Central has lost roughly a third of its volume since November.” This feels presumptuous the first few times you do it, because a title that states a conclusion is committing to something in a way that a descriptive title never has to. That commitment is the point. If you cannot write the sentence, the honest reading is not that the chart is fine and the title is hard — it is that you do not yet know what the chart is for.

The grammar of graphics, in one idea

There is a genuinely useful piece of theory underneath all of this, and it takes about a minute to explain. Every statistical chart, however elaborate, is the same three decisions:

  1. Data — which rows.
  2. Aesthetics — which variable maps to position, colour, size, shape.
  3. Geometry — what marks get drawn: points, lines, bars, areas.

That is the grammar of graphics, and it is the idea ggplot2 in R is built on, which is why its code reads the way it does. Change the geometry and hold everything else fixed and you have a different chart of the same data: the same rows, the same variable on the same axis, drawn as points rather than as bars.

The practical value is that “which chart should I use?” stops being a matter of taste and becomes a question with structure. You are not choosing from a gallery of chart types; you are deciding what maps to position — the encoding people read most accurately — and then deciding what marks to draw. Someone who has this idea can invent a chart form they have never seen, and, more usefully, can say precisely what is wrong with one they have been handed.

The code below draws the regional series from Tuesday’s output. Read it for its structure rather than its syntax: a data step, then a set of mappings, then geometry, then the labelling that carries the argument.

  • R (ggplot2)
  • Python (matplotlib)
library(tidyverse)

weekly <- arrow::read_parquet("outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet")

regional <- weekly |>
  group_by(region, week_starting) |>
  summarise(units = sum(units_sold), .groups = "drop")

ggplot(regional, aes(week_starting, units, colour = region)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.7) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = as.Date("2025-11-03"),
             linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey40") +
  annotate("text", x = as.Date("2025-11-10"), y = Inf, hjust = 0, vjust = 1.4,
           label = "Northern re-segmentation", size = 3.2, colour = "grey30") +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("#1F2A66", "#0B5E82", "#D99A2B",
                                 "#1C7293", "#D99A2B")) +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::comma) +
  labs(title = "North Central has lost roughly a third of its weekly volume since November",
       subtitle = "Weekly units sold by region, Jan 2024 – Mar 2026",
       x = NULL, y = "Units per week", colour = NULL,
       caption = "Source: sell-out data, deduplicated. Stockout weeks counted as zero.") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) +
  theme(legend.position = "top",
        plot.title = element_text(face = "bold"),
        panel.grid.minor = element_blank())
import pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt

weekly = pd.read_parquet("outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet")
regional = (weekly.groupby(["region", "week_starting"], as_index=False)
                  .units_sold.sum())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4.5))
palette = ["#1F2A66", "#0B5E82", "#D99A2B", "#1C7293", "#D99A2B"]
for (name, grp), colour in zip(regional.groupby("region"), palette):
    ax.plot(grp.week_starting, grp.units_sold, label=name, color=colour, lw=1.4)

ax.axvline(pd.Timestamp("2025-11-03"), ls="--", color="0.5")
ax.annotate("Northern re-segmentation", (pd.Timestamp("2025-11-10"), ax.get_ylim()[1]),
            va="top", fontsize=9, color="0.3")
ax.set_title("North Central has lost roughly a third of its weekly volume since November",
             loc="left", weight="bold")
ax.set_ylabel("Units per week"); ax.legend(frameon=False, ncol=5)
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)

Three details in those blocks carry more of the argument than the chart type does. The vertical dashed line and its annotation name the event, so that the reader is not left to infer why the line moved. The caption states how stockout weeks were treated, which is a modelling decision disclosed on the face of the chart rather than buried in an appendix. And the title makes the claim, so that a reader who sees only the slide still leaves with the finding.

The bar chart of group means, and what it conceals

The most common chart in business is a set of bars showing the average of some quantity for each of several groups. It is popular for good reasons: it is easy to produce, easy to read, and it answers the question people usually ask, which is “which group is highest?”

The difficulty is that it shows one number per group and hides everything that produced the number. To see how much that matters, consider four datasets that all yield an identical set of bars.

In the first, the groups genuinely differ: every observation in one group really is higher than almost every observation in another. In the second, three of the groups are ordinary and one is held up by a handful of extreme values, so that its mean describes none of its members. In the third, the distributions overlap so heavily that the differences between the group averages are not differences you would act on. In the fourth, one group is bimodal — split into a high cluster and a low one, with the mean sitting in the empty space between them, describing a case that does not occur.

The bars are the same in all four. The decisions you would take are not.

None of this makes the bar chart a bad chart. It makes it a chart that answers a narrower question than the one people typically bring to it. What to draw instead, in increasing order of information carried:

  • Points, jittered — if you have fewer than a few hundred observations, simply show them. Nudging each point sideways at random (“jitter”) stops them landing on top of one another.
  • Box plot — the median as a line, the middle half of the data as a box, the extremes as whiskers, and anything unusual as individual points. Compact, honest, and readable by anybody once you have explained it once.
  • Violin, or violin plus box — the actual shape of the distribution, mirrored around its axis, which is the only one of these forms that shows bimodality unambiguously.
  • Bars with intervals — if the audience expects bars, at least let the bars carry the uncertainty, so that overlapping ranges tell the reader when a difference is not solid.

The two-second rule

An audience gives a chart roughly two seconds before they either understand it or stop trying and listen to you instead. This is not impatience; it is what happens when a slide appears while somebody is already speaking. Design for two seconds and everything else follows.

In practice that means: sorted by size rather than alphabetically. Labelled directly on the series, rather than through a legend the reader has to match by colour. One colour, unless colour is carrying meaning. The claim in the title. And the two things you want compared placed physically next to each other, because a comparison across a page is a comparison the reader has to hold in memory.

There is one further point that is easy to miss and does more work than the rest combined: the quantity you want compared should be positioned along a common baseline. Human eyes judge the lengths of things starting from the same line very accurately, and judge angles, areas and colour intensities poorly — a finding from a long line of graphical perception experiments that has held up consistently. That single fact explains why sorted bars beat pie charts, why stacked bars stop working beyond two or three segments (only the bottom segment shares a baseline), and why “which of these is bigger?” should almost never be answered with a treemap.

Colour, and doing it once

Pick a palette once, use it everywhere, and make sure a given category is the same colour in every chart in the pack. In a fifty-page board document, that consistency does more for comprehension than any individual chart improvement, because it lets a reader carry a meaning from page eleven to page thirty-four without re-reading a legend.

Three rules matter more than aesthetics.

Check it in greyscale. Somebody will print it, or project it through a tired bulb, and a palette that relies on hue alone collapses into a set of indistinguishable greys.

Do not encode meaning in red-versus-green alone. Red-green colour vision deficiency affects several per cent of men — around 8% in northern European populations and lower, roughly 2–6%, in most African studies. Either way, in a large cohort it is likely to be somebody in the room. The fix is not to abandon red and green but to make them redundant: carry the same meaning in position, shape or a written label as well, so that the chart still works when the colour does not.

Reserve one loud colour for the thing you are arguing about, and make everything else grey. A single highlighted series against a muted background is the highest-impact change available to most charts, and it costs one line of code. It also enforces the discipline from the start of this session, because you cannot highlight one series until you have decided which series the argument is about.

# The whole programme's palette, defined once.
tdbg_colours <- c(indigo = "#1F2A66", blue = "#0B5E82", cyan = "#D99A2B",
                  teal   = "#1C7293", gold = "#D99A2B", grey = "#B6BDCB")

# Highlight one series, mute the rest.
ggplot(regional, aes(week_starting, units, group = region,
                     colour = region == "North Central")) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.7) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(`TRUE` = "#D99A2B", `FALSE` = "#D5DAE3"),
                      guide = "none")

The trick in that second block is worth noting, because it is not obvious the first time you see it. Rather than mapping colour to region, which would give five colours, it maps colour to the test region == "North Central" — a column of TRUE and FALSE. Two values, two colours, and the chart now argues rather than displays.

The chart that did not land

Most people who present regularly have a chart somewhere that did not do what they hoped: the room went quiet in the wrong way, or the questions came back about something other than the point. Bring one of those to this session. They are far more instructive than a chart that worked, and the reason it did not land is almost never carelessness — it is usually a mismatch between the claim and the form of exactly the kind this session is about.

The diagnosis is nearly always one of five things.

  1. No claim. The title described the data instead of the finding, so the audience had to locate the argument themselves and different people located different ones.
  2. Wrong comparison. You showed levels when the audience needed change, or change when they needed levels. Both charts are correct; only one answers the question in the room.
  3. Too many series. Beyond about five lines, nobody reads any of them, including the one that mattered. Small multiples — the same small chart repeated once per category, on shared axes — solve this better than any amount of colour work.
  4. Uncertainty hidden. You showed a point estimate, were asked “how sure are you?”, and had no visual answer, so the conversation moved to whether the number could be trusted rather than to what to do about it. 3.3 is largely about this.
  5. Wrong denominator. A rate where an absolute was needed, or the reverse. “Churn is up two points” and “we lost 240 customers” are the same fact and land completely differently, and which one is right depends entirely on what the audience is being asked to decide.

Core lab · 30 minutes

  • Take three charts from a real pack of yours. Write the claim each one is making, as a sentence, before looking at it again.
  • For each, decide whether the form supports the claim. Rebuild the two that do not.
  • Rewrite every title as its claim.
  • Build one Pareto chart of your own data — the sorted bars plus the cumulative line. Find out what share of your revenue comes from your top decile.
  • Sceptic: for each rebuilt chart, state the opposite conclusion and see whether the chart rules it out. If it does not, the chart is not finished.

Do the first item from memory, before you open the file. Writing down what you believe the chart says and then discovering what it actually shows is a small, private and unusually efficient exercise, and it is the reason the instruction says “before looking at it again”.

Stretch

Build the small multiples version of the regional chart: one small panel per region, same axes throughout, sorted by size.

Then argue with your table about which is better — the five-line chart or the twelve small panels. There is a right answer, and it depends entirely on whether the claim is about comparison between regions or behaviour within each one. A single set of overlaid lines is excellent for comparison and poor for detail, because the lines obscure one another; small multiples are the reverse. Working out which claim you are making is the exercise, and the disagreement at the table is usually a disagreement about that rather than about charts.

Copilot check

Assistants write chart code well and make chart decisions badly, in a specific and predictable way: asked for “a chart of this data”, they produce whatever is most common in their training material, which is a bar chart of group means, with a legend, and a title that describes the data. That is a reasonable answer to an underspecified request, and the fix is to stop underspecifying.

Two prompts do most of it:

Here is my data and here is the single claim I want the chart to make: “[claim]”. Propose three chart forms that would support that claim, say what each one hides, and recommend one. Then write the code with the claim as the title and direct labels rather than a legend.

and, afterwards:

Look at this chart specification. What conclusion could a reader draw from it that the underlying data does not support?

The second is the Sceptic’s question, delegated, and it works surprisingly well. Critiquing a specific artefact is a far easier task than auditing an open situation, which is exactly why it is worth handing over — and why the judgement about what the chart is for stays with you.

Takeaways

  1. Write the claim as a sentence, then choose the form. The title is the claim.
  2. Data, aesthetics, geometry. Three decisions, every chart.
  3. A bar of means hides the four situations that would change your decision.
  4. Compare along a common baseline. Sort. Label directly. One loud colour.
  5. Design for two seconds.
