The KPI tree

From a P&L number to something somebody can change — Session 1.2, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can decompose a financial outcome into a tree of drivers, identify which nodes are actually controllable by a named person, write a definition of your top-line metric that survives cross-examination, and spot the two failure modes that destroy most KPI frameworks: the metric nobody owns, and the metric two departments define differently.

Why a tree, and why it matters more now

A familiar sequence: an executive team asks for “a dashboard of our KPIs”, and what arrives is a page of forty numbers. Each number is defensible on its own. Together they are not a management system, because nothing on the page says which number causes which, or which of them anybody could move by Friday.

A tree is the alternative, and the idea is simple. You start from one number that the business is genuinely judged on — gross profit, cost-to-income, contribution per customer — and you break it into the components that produce it. Where you can, you break it multiplicatively: revenue is units multiplied by average price, so a 3% improvement in either flows through to revenue in a way you can compute rather than argue about. You keep going until you reach nodes that a specific person can change through a specific action. Everything between the top of the tree and those nodes is arithmetic, and arithmetic is auditable: anyone can check it, and nobody has to take it on trust.

Why this matters more in 2026 than it did in 2016 is worth a sentence, because it changes where the effort goes. When computing a single new metric meant three days of an analyst’s time, the tree was rationed by effort — you measured what you could afford to measure. That cost has collapsed. What rations the tree now is clarity of thought, which moves the hard question from “can we measure this?” to “should this be on the tree at all?”

A worked tree: the FMCG distributor

Take the distributor whose data sits in the data room. Gross profit is the number the board sees. Here is what it decomposes into.

Read the tree from the bottom rather than the top. The green leaves are the only nodes anybody can actually act on; everything above them is a consequence of what happens at the leaves. Nobody wakes up and improves gross profit. They list two more SKUs in Kano, or move a reorder point, or decline a promotion — and gross profit changes as a result.

Follow one path to see how the arithmetic behaves. Units sold is distribution multiplied by rate of sale multiplied by availability. Because those three multiply, lifting availability by one percentage point lifts units by about one per cent on that line — with no extra listings, no promotion and no price change, and without anybody having to be persuaded to buy more. That is a small gain that arrives every week and costs almost nothing to hold on to, which is the characteristic profile of a good analytics target.

A tree without the next column is decoration, so attach owners and actions:

Leaf Owner The action Cadence
Distribution National sales manager Listings drive per depot Monthly
Rate of sale Trade marketing Promotion plan, shelf position Monthly
Availability Supply planner Reorder point, safety stock Weekly
List price Commercial director Price file Quarterly
Discount depth Trade marketing Promo calendar approval Weekly
Unit cost Procurement Supplier negotiation, consolidation Quarterly
Obsolescence Depot managers FEFO discipline, markdown triggers Weekly

Now look at the cadence column, because it is doing more work than it appears to. A decision taken weekly is taken fifty-two times a year, so an improvement of 5% in the quality of that decision is banked fifty-two times; the same 5% on a quarterly decision is banked four times. This is why the weekly leaves are where analytics earns its return, and why the quarterly ones usually improve through negotiation, relationships and judgement instead. It is not that a model could not help with a quarterly price file. It is that you will wait a year to find out whether it did.

The definition fight

This is where a great many KPI frameworks quietly come apart, and it happens for understandable reasons rather than careless ones.

Open bank_customers.csv. It carries two different churn flags, both created in good faith by different parts of the bank. Run the comparison yourself in whichever language you are most comfortable with — the three below do the same thing.

  • SQL
  • R
  • Python
SELECT
  ROUND(AVG(churned_90d), 4)  AS risk_definition,
  ROUND(AVG(dormant_flag), 4) AS finance_definition,
  SUM(CASE WHEN churned_90d <> dormant_flag THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS disagreements,
  COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM 'bank_customers.csv';
library(tidyverse)

bank <- read_csv("data/bank_customers.csv")

bank |>
  summarise(
    risk_definition    = mean(churned_90d),
    finance_definition = mean(dormant_flag),
    disagreements      = sum(churned_90d != dormant_flag),
    customers          = n()
  )
import pandas as pd

bank = pd.read_csv("data/bank_customers.csv")

print({
    "risk_definition":    bank.churned_90d.mean().round(4),
    "finance_definition": bank.dormant_flag.mean().round(4),
    "disagreements":      int((bank.churned_90d != bank.dormant_flag).sum()),
    "customers":          len(bank),
})
What you will see, and what it means

Risk says 10.6% of the base churned. Finance says 15.9%. The two flags disagree about 633 customers — roughly five points of the base, which in a retention conversation is the difference between a comfortable year and an emergency.

Neither definition is wrong, and this is the part worth sitting with. Risk counts accounts that were formally closed, because a closed account is the event their models are built to predict. Finance counts accounts with no qualifying activity, because an account that generates nothing is worth nothing to finance whether or not anybody ever filed the paperwork. Each department has defined the word to mean the thing that matters to them.

The difficulty is not the disagreement itself but the fact that both numbers circulate, both are called “churn”, and the retention budget may be sized from one of them while the retention target is tracked against the other. When that happens the team can hit its target and miss the outcome, or miss its target while doing everything right. This is a governance question rather than a data question, and it is among the most common findings in a first analytics review — which is another way of saying it is normal, and fixable.

The fix is not technical either. It is one page per metric: the metric, its owner, its exact definition, the source system, and the date the definition last changed. Fifteen minutes to write, and it prevents a surprising number of long arguments.

The definition card

For every node on your tree that anybody outside your team will see, write a card like this one:

Metric
90-day customer churn
Owner
Head of Retention (single named person, not a department)
Definition
A customer is churned in month m if their account status is Closed at any point in m, or they have had zero qualifying transactions for 90 consecutive days ending in m. Qualifying excludes fee reversals and internal transfers.
Source
core_banking.accounts + core_banking.transactions, nightly extract
Excludes
Accounts opened within the period; deceased; staff accounts
Changed
2026-02-14 — added the transaction condition; restated 24 months of history

The Excludes and Changed lines are the ones most often left off, and they are the two that matter most. Excludes is where two honest teams diverge without noticing — one of them counts staff accounts and the other does not, and the gap is small enough to look like noise until somebody builds a target on it. Changed is where the argument goes when the number moves: if a definition was revised in February and the history was restated, then a jump in February is your own doing rather than the market’s, and the card is what lets you say so in the meeting rather than the week after it.

Real or nominal — the fastest credibility win in the room

Here is a check that takes seconds and changes how a number is read. Run this against the distributor’s sell-out data.

  • SQL
  • R
SELECT
  EXTRACT(year FROM week_starting) AS yr,
  ROUND(SUM(revenue_ngn) / 1e9, 2) AS revenue_bn,
  ROUND(SUM(units_sold)  / 1e6, 2) AS units_m
FROM 'fmcg_sell_out.csv'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
library(tidyverse)

read_csv("data/fmcg_sell_out.csv") |>
  mutate(yr = lubridate::year(week_starting)) |>
  group_by(yr) |>
  summarise(revenue_bn = sum(revenue_ngn, na.rm = TRUE) / 1e9,
            units_m    = sum(units_sold,  na.rm = TRUE) / 1e6) |>
  mutate(across(where(is.numeric), \(x) round(x, 2)))

Nominal revenue grows 42.8% from 2024 to 2025. Units grow 8.5%. Average headline inflation over 2025 was 25.3%.

Set those three figures side by side and the story changes shape. The business sold about a twelfth more product and took roughly two-fifths more money for it, in a year when prices in general rose by about a quarter. As a rough check you can do in your head: 1.428 divided by 1.253 is about 1.14, so the real increase is nearer a seventh than two-fifths. That is still growth, and it is still worth having — but “revenue up 42.8%” and “we sold 8.5% more and the naira did the rest” are two different sentences, and only one of them survives a question from the board.

There is a second adjustment hiding underneath, and it is the sort of thing that is invisible until somebody looks. 2024 contains fifty-three ISO Mondays and 2025 contains fifty-two, so the two years are not the same length in the only unit this data has. Put the figures on a per-week basis and they become +45.6% and +10.6% — both larger, because the later year had one fewer week in which to earn. The calendar was quietly making the headline look worse than it was, which is the opposite of the direction people expect.

Look at the chart with that arithmetic in mind. The deflated revenue line and the units line finish close together, which is what you would expect if the business is selling somewhat more of much the same mix. The distance up to the nominal line is the currency.

The lesson, stated plainly

In a high-inflation economy, a nominal series carries two stories at once — what happened to the business, and what happened to the money — and it reports them as though they were one. Separating them is not an advanced technique. It is one division, and it is the difference between a growth story and a currency story.

So ask “real or nominal?” before you ask anything else about a series. It takes four seconds, it is asked far less often than you would expect, and in a Nigerian board room in 2026 it is the fastest way to establish that you know what you are looking at.

Then ask the harder follow-up: deflated by what? Headline inflation is a national average across a basket that has very little to do with a beverages business whose costs are dominated by imported concentrate and packaging. Food inflation is closer to the truth for such a business; the exchange rate may be closer still. There is no universally correct answer here — the choice of deflator is a judgement, which is precisely why it belongs on the chart, stated, where a reader can disagree with it.

The three failure modes

The metric nobody owns. If the owner is recorded as “the business” or “the exec team”, then when the metric moves, everybody assumes somebody else is looking at it. Each node needs one name — not two. If two people genuinely share it, that is worth knowing in itself: you have found an organisational design question, and a dashboard will not settle it.

The metric two departments define differently. This is the case above. The cost is subtle: it is not that people are confused, it is that every discussion of the metric turns into a discussion about the metric rather than about the business. Two capable teams can spend a quarter arbitrating a definition that a single page would have settled.

The metric that is measured because it is easy. This third one is less often discussed and just as expensive. NPS survives in many organisations not because anyone has shown it predicts behaviour in that particular business, but because it is cheap to collect and everybody else reports it. That is not a reason to drop it — it may well be earning its place — but it is a reason to ask the test question before a metric goes on the tree: what decision changes if this moves by five points? If nothing changes, the metric is information rather than a KPI, and it can live somewhere other than the tree.

Core lab · 30 minutes

  • Draw the KPI tree for your own P&L or function. Two levels minimum, three if the decomposition is genuinely multiplicative.
  • Mark every leaf that a named person can change, and write the name.
  • Write the cadence next to each. Circle every weekly one — that is your analytics shortlist.
  • Write a full definition card for the top node.
  • Ask a colleague from a different function to state the same definition without seeing yours. Note where you differ.

The last step takes about four minutes and is the point of the whole lab. Differences found in a classroom cost nothing; the same differences found in a board pack cost a quarter.

Stretch

Find the node on your tree that is measured in nominal currency and rebuild it in real terms using ng_macro_monthly.csv as the deflator. Then answer the question that follows: does the story your last board pack told still hold once the currency has been taken out of it? Sometimes it does, and that is a genuinely good thing to be able to demonstrate.

Second stretch: find the node on your tree that somebody else in this room would define differently, and go and have the argument now, while it is free.

Copilot check

Ask the assistant to build a KPI tree from a description of your business. It will produce something plausible and well-organised in about twenty seconds, and as a first draft that is genuinely useful — it will remind you of branches you would have forgotten.

Then check three things that are almost always wrong in that first draft, for reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of the model:

  1. It does not know your definitions, so it will assume the textbook ones. Your business almost certainly defines at least one of those terms differently, and the tree inherits the error silently.
  2. It will mix additive and multiplicative decompositions, sometimes producing a sum where your business has a product or the reverse. This matters more than it sounds: in a multiplicative branch, a 5% improvement at one node flows straight through to the parent, while in an additive branch it flows through only in proportion to that node’s share.
  3. It will not put owners on the leaves, because it has no idea who works for you — and the owner column is the one that turns a diagram into a management tool.

The tree the assistant gives you is a good first draft and a poor final answer, and that sentence generalises to almost everything this week.

Takeaways

  1. Decompose until you reach nodes with a named owner and a specific action. Stop there.
  2. Weekly-cadence leaves are where analytics compounds. Quarterly leaves are where negotiation compounds.
  3. Write the definition card. The Excludes and Changed lines are the important ones.
  4. Ask “real or nominal?” first, then “deflated by what?”
