LLM classification at scale

A classifier is a measurement instrument. Calibrate it — Session 4.2, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can classify a whole corpus for theme, sentiment and intent in a few lines; you can validate the classifier against hand-labelled truth and state its error rate; and you can decide whether the work should run in the cloud or entirely on your own machine — which is a legal question before it is a technical one.

The shift, and the discipline it requires

Three years ago, classifying free text by theme meant assembling a labelled training set of several thousand examples, holding a taxonomy argument that ran for a month, and finding a data scientist with time to do it. The economics of that ruled the approach out for most questions, which is why so much free text was left uncounted.

Now you write the categories in a sentence, run the corpus through, and have a labelled table in an afternoon. It works, it costs very little, and — as 4.1 argued — it handles Pidgin, code-switching and misspellings far better than any lexicon. That last point is why this session exists rather than being an appendix to the previous one.

It is not uniformly good, and the honest account of where it is weak matters more than the enthusiasm. Sarcasm remains the standing weak point of automated sentiment: “wonderful, another failed transfer” is scored positive by a great many systems, including capable ones. Published benchmarks on Nigerian Pidgin and other African languages — NaijaSenti, AfriSenti — consistently show accuracy below English on the same task, which stands to reason given what these models were trained on. Neither is a reason to avoid the approach. Both are reasons to measure it separately for the languages in your corpus, rather than quoting one overall accuracy figure that averages a strong result on the English rows with a weaker one on the rest.

That leads to the danger this session is built around. The old approach was expensive, and its expense acted as an accidental quality gate: nobody assembled three thousand labelled examples without also thinking hard about what the categories meant. The new approach removes the cost, and with it the gate. It is now trivially easy to produce a confident-looking distribution of themes without ever finding out whether the classifier is any good.

The rule for this session

An LLM classifier is a measurement instrument. Every measurement instrument has to be calibrated against something.

A classifier converts something you cannot count into something you can, and like any instrument that does that, the conversion has a tolerance. Nobody signs off a laboratory result without its error bars; this is the same object.

If you have not hand-labelled two hundred rows and compared, you do not have an error rate. And if you do not have an error rate, you do not have a measurement — you have an opinion that was expensive to generate.

Two hundred rows takes about forty minutes. It is the difference between “sentiment improved by four points” being a finding and being noise.

What the model can and cannot be trusted to do

Before writing any code it is worth being precise about the shape of this tool, because its strengths and weaknesses are not quite where intuition puts them.

It is reliable at recognition. Given a review and a fixed list of categories, deciding which one the review is about is close to what these models do natively. It reads “transfer failed but they debited me” and returns billing for much the same reason a person would — in Pidgin, in English, and in a sentence that switches between the two halfway through. It is untroubled by misspellings, missing punctuation and the compressed register people use on a phone.

It is unreliable at anything requiring knowledge it was not given. It does not know your product names, your internal process boundaries, or that “the Tuesday run” means the replenishment cycle. If the distinction between two of your categories depends on institutional knowledge, the model cannot make it — and it will not tell you that it could not.

It is weakest where humans are also weak, but it fails differently. Sarcasm, irony and mixed sentiment are genuinely hard, and two careful human labellers will disagree on them too. The difference is that a human labeller says “I am not sure about this one”. The model does not.

The failure mode to design against is the confident wrong label

Consider what a classical lexicon does with a sentence it does not understand. It finds no scoring word, returns neutral or nothing, and the row shows up as unclassified. The gap is visible; you can count it, and its size tells you how far to trust the rest.

A language model given an enum of seven categories returns one of those seven for every row it is shown, whatever the row contains. There is no “I do not know” in the schema unless you put one there. A review it has misread therefore does not arrive marked as doubtful — it arrives as branch_service, in the same column, indistinguishable from the rows it got right. The errors are silent, and silent errors do not get investigated.

Two further properties make this worse than it first looks:

  1. The errors are not random, so they do not average out. If the classifier is weaker on Pidgin than on English, every theme those customers disproportionately raise is understated — consistently, in the same direction, every quarter. Random error shrinks as the corpus grows. Systematic error does not shrink at all; a bigger corpus simply gives you a more precise estimate of the wrong number.
  2. Nothing downstream can recover it. Once the label is in the table, every chart and trend built on top inherits it, and each step makes the number look more solid. By the time it reaches a slide it has acquired a decimal place and a comparison against last quarter.

None of this argues against using the tool. It argues for the one countermeasure that works: hold some rows out and check them by hand. No diagnostic computed from the model’s own output, and no self-assessment you can ask it for, substitutes for a sample somebody labelled independently.

Classifying the corpus

  • R (mall — runs locally)
  • R (ellmer — any provider)
  • Python

mall applies a local language model to a data frame column, row by row. Nothing leaves the machine, which for confidential text is frequently the difference between doing the work and not doing it.

library(tidyverse)
library(mall)

# Point at a locally-running model. No data leaves this laptop.
llm_use("ollama", "llama3.2", seed = 42, .cache = "_llm_cache")

reviews <- read_csv("data/customer_reviews.csv")

labelled <- reviews |>
  llm_sentiment(review_text, options = c("positive", "neutral", "negative")) |>
  llm_classify(review_text,
               labels = c("billing", "app_reliability", "network_quality",
                          "branch_service", "delivery", "pricing", "other"),
               pred_name = "theme") |>
  llm_extract(review_text,
              labels = "the single product or service the customer is talking about",
              pred_name = "subject")

labelled |> count(theme, .sentiment) |> pivot_wider(names_from = .sentiment, values_from = n)
library(ellmer)

chat <- chat_anthropic(system_prompt = paste(
  "You classify customer reviews for a Nigerian bank.",
  "Reviews may be in English or Nigerian Pidgin.",
  "Return only the requested structured fields."
))

schema <- type_object(
  theme     = type_enum(values = c("billing", "app_reliability", "network_quality",
                                   "branch_service", "delivery", "pricing", "other")),
  sentiment = type_enum(values = c("positive", "neutral", "negative")),
  intent    = type_enum(values = c("complaint", "praise", "question",
                                   "churn_threat", "other")),
  severity  = type_integer(description = "1 = trivial, 5 = would leave over this")
)

classify_one <- function(txt) chat$chat_structured(txt, type = schema)

# Batch, cache, and retry. Do not run 5,000 rows in a naive loop.
labelled <- reviews |>
  mutate(result = map(review_text, possibly(classify_one, otherwise = NULL))) |>
  unnest_wider(result)
import pandas as pd, json, time, pathlib, anthropic
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
SCHEMA = {"theme": ["billing","app_reliability","network_quality","branch_service",
                    "delivery","pricing","other"],
          "sentiment": ["positive","neutral","negative"],
          "intent": ["complaint","praise","question","churn_threat","other"]}

PROMPT = (
    "Classify this customer review for a Nigerian bank. It may be in English or "
    "Nigerian Pidgin. Return ONLY JSON with keys theme, sentiment, intent, severity "
    "(1-5). Allowed values: " + json.dumps(SCHEMA) + "\n\nReview: "
)

MODEL = "claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929"      # PIN the dated version, not the alias


def _extract_json(txt):
    """Models add prose. Take the JSON object and nothing else."""
    i, j = txt.find("{"), txt.rfind("}")
    if i == -1 or j == -1:
        raise ValueError(f"no JSON in response: {txt[:80]}")
    return json.loads(txt[i:j + 1])


def classify(text, tries=4):
    for k in range(tries):
        try:
            r = client.messages.create(
                model=MODEL, max_tokens=150, temperature=0,   # temperature=0
                messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT + text}])
            return _extract_json(r.content[0].text)
        except Exception:
            if k == tries - 1:
                return {"theme": "other", "sentiment": "neutral",
                        "intent": "other", "error": True}
            time.sleep(2 ** k)                                 # backoff


reviews = pd.read_csv("data/customer_reviews.csv")
out, path = [], pathlib.Path("outputs/labels.jsonl")
done = {json.loads(l)["review_id"] for l in path.open()} if path.exists() else set()
with path.open("a") as fh, ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=6) as pool:
    todo = reviews[~reviews.review_id.isin(done)]              # resume, don't restart
    for rid, res in zip(todo.review_id, pool.map(classify, todo.review_text)):
        fh.write(json.dumps({"review_id": rid, **res}) + "\n")  # write incrementally

All three do the same thing — send each review to a model with a fixed list of permitted answers, and write the answer back as a column. They differ only in where the model runs and how much of the machinery you build yourself.

Four operational things that will bite you

Cache. You will re-run this — to fix a bug, to add a category, to answer a question somebody asked in a meeting. Without a cache you pay twice and, worse, you get slightly different answers the second time, and then you cannot reproduce your own chart. Cache on a hash of the input text, so identical text always yields the stored label.

Constrain the output. Use a schema or an enum — a list of permitted values the model must choose from. Ask for a free-text category instead and you will receive billing, Billing, billing issue and payment/billing as four distinct categories, and spend an hour reconciling them into one.

Pin the model version and set temperature to zero. “We used the model” is not a method. “We used model X, dated version Y, at temperature 0” is. When the number moves next quarter you need to be able to say whether the business changed or the instrument did — and an alias like claude-sonnet-4-5 points at different weights over time, which is exactly the ambiguity you are trying to remove. Temperature is the setting that controls how much randomness goes into the model’s choice of words; at zero it makes the most likely choice every time.

Note that not every API offers a seed parameter, and temperature 0 is not a guarantee of determinism. The cache is your reproducibility mechanism, not the seed: hash the input text, store the label, and never re-classify a row you have already classified.

Batch and retry. Five thousand sequential calls will take an hour and fail somewhere in the middle. Parallelise modestly, retry with backoff, and write results incrementally — the .jsonl file in the Python tab exists so that an interrupted run resumes rather than restarts.

Validation — the forty minutes that make it real

This is the section that separates a measurement from an assertion, and it is the one most often skipped — usually because the results already look plausible and there is a meeting on Thursday.

The procedure has four steps. First, draw a random sample — two hundred rows, not twenty. There is arithmetic behind that number rather than tradition: if the classifier agrees with a human on roughly 88% of rows, two hundred rows will contain about 200 × 0.12 = 24 disagreements, which is enough to see a pattern in. Forty rows would yield about five, which tells you whether something is catastrophically wrong but not where the boundary problems are.

Second, label them by hand, blind. A human reads the review and assigns a category without seeing what the model said. This matters more than it sounds: shown the model’s answer first, a labeller agrees with it far more often than they otherwise would. The phenomenon is called anchoring, it is not a weakness of the individual, and the remedy is to strip the model’s column out of the file before anybody opens it.

Third, compare. Fourth — and this is the step that repays the effort — look at where the disagreements fall rather than only at how many there are.

set.seed(1)
gold <- labelled |>
  slice_sample(n = 200) |>
  select(review_id, review_text) |>
  mutate(human_theme = NA_character_, human_sentiment = NA_character_)

write_csv(gold, "outputs/validation_blank.csv")
# --- a human fills this in, without seeing the model's answer ---

gold <- read_csv("outputs/validation_filled.csv") |>
  left_join(labelled |> select(review_id, theme, .sentiment), by = "review_id")

# Overall agreement
gold |> summarise(theme_agreement     = mean(theme == human_theme),
                  sentiment_agreement = mean(.sentiment == human_sentiment))

# The confusion matrix is where the useful information is
gold |> count(human_theme, theme) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = theme, values_from = n, values_fill = 0)

The first summarise() produces raw agreement — the share of rows on which model and human chose the same label. It is the obvious number to report, and it is not sufficient alone.

Do not report raw agreement without a floor to compare it against. With seven categories and the largest holding 21% of the corpus, a classifier that ignored the text entirely and guessed the majority class every time would score 21% while knowing nothing at all — and on a more concentrated corpus, where one theme holds half the rows, that floor is far higher.

Cohen’s kappa does that comparison for you. It asks how much of the agreement you observed is more than chance would have produced, expressed as a share of how much room for improvement there was: κ = (observed − chance) ÷ (1 − chance). A kappa of 0 means the classifier is doing no better than guessing; 1 means perfect agreement. It is one line:

irr::kappa2(gold[, c("human_theme", "theme")])

The arithmetic is worth following once. Suppose observed agreement comes out at 0.88 and the agreement chance alone would produce, given how the seven categories are distributed, comes out at 0.20. Then κ = (0.88 − 0.20) ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 0.68 ÷ 0.80 = 0.85 — the classifier captured 85% of the improvement available over guessing. Quoting that alongside the raw 88% tells a reviewer you understood the difference.

Then read the confusion matrix, not the headline number. A confusion matrix is a table with human labels down the side and model labels across the top, so each cell counts the rows where the human said one thing and the model said another. 88% agreement sounds fine; the matrix answers the more useful question of where the 12% went.

The typical pattern in this corpus is that billing and app_reliability get confused, because “the app debited me twice” is genuinely both — a payment failure that happened inside the app. That is not a model failure. It is a taxonomy failure: your two categories overlap, the model is being asked to choose between them on a review that satisfies both, and two human labellers would split on it as well. The fix is not a better model. Either merge the themes or add a rule to the prompt saying which wins when both apply — and say in the method note which you did.

Carry the distinction beyond this example. Disagreements scattered evenly across the matrix suggest the classifier is struggling. Disagreements piled into one or two cells suggest your categories are, and that is a problem you fix with a definition rather than with technology.

What to put in the paper

Themes were assigned by [model, dated version] at temperature 0 against a fixed seven-category taxonomy; labels were cached, and the cache is included with this analysis. A random sample of 200 reviews, stratified by language, was independently hand-labelled without sight of the model’s answer. Model and human agreed on 88% of theme assignments (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0.85) and 94% of sentiment. Disagreements concentrated on the billing / app-reliability boundary, where the review describes a payment failure inside the app; these two themes are therefore reported together. 8.6% of the corpus is in Nigerian Pidgin, where agreement is lower than in English; the sample contains too few Pidgin rows to state a separate error rate with confidence, and a stratified follow-up is planned.

Four sentences, and each does one job: the first makes the run reproducible, the second establishes that the check was independent, the third states the error rate and where it concentrates, and the fourth admits a limitation the reader could otherwise have found for themselves — which is the sentence that earns the other three their credibility.

It converts “the model said” into a measurement with a stated error, and it is the difference between a technical reviewer trusting your paper and quietly discounting it.

Where it runs, and why that is a board-level question

There are three places this work can happen, and the choice between them is a legal question before it is a technical one.

Where the data goes Speed Quality Use when
Hosted model Leaves your network, often the country Fast Best The text is not personal or confidential
Local model Nowhere Slower Good, and improving fast Customer records, HR text, anything under the NDPA
Enterprise / in-region deployment Your tenancy Fast Best Your organisation has procured it and DPIA’d it

For 4,938 anonymised course reviews any of the three is fine, which is why you can run today’s lab without a conversation with anybody.

Real call-centre notes are a different matter. They contain names, account numbers and the details of individual complaints, and sending them to a model hosted outside the country is a cross-border personal data transfer under the NDPA 2023, which needs a lawful basis established before anybody runs the script rather than discovered afterwards. That is where a promising internal project most often stops, and it stops late, after the work has been done.

The practical answer that unblocks most teams is to run it locally. A local model on a decent laptop classifies a few thousand short texts in an hour or two, costs nothing per call, works without internet, and never raises the transfer question at all. It is somewhat less accurate — and for theme classification against a fixed taxonomy, the gap is usually smaller than the gap between two human labellers doing the same job. That is the comparison to make when somebody asks whether the local option is good enough: good enough compared to what?

We come back to this properly in 5.4.

Core lab · 30 minutes

A classification pass over 4,938 reviews has already been run and cached — it is data/customer_reviews_labelled.csv, with the model and temperature recorded in the file. A whole room classifying five thousand rows each, simultaneously, on conference wifi, is an hour of waiting and nothing else. The lab is about validation.

  • Classify fifty rows yourself so you have run the mechanics once: enum, schema, temperature zero, cache.
  • Join the cached labels to the corpus. Produce the theme-by-quarter volume table. That table is the deliverable, not the classifier.
  • Hand-label forty rows as a table, blind — do not look at the model’s answer first. Stratify: make sure some are Pidgin.
  • Compute agreement, and then Cohen’s kappa, and build the confusion matrix.
  • Report per-class recall, not just the overall rate. Find the class the classifier is worst on, and ask what it costs you to miss it.
  • Find the category boundary causing most of the disagreement. Fix the taxonomy or the prompt, and say which.
  • Write the four-sentence method paragraph.

Per-class recall, in the fifth item, is the share of rows genuinely belonging to a category that the classifier actually assigned to it. It matters because a small theme can be missed almost entirely without moving the overall agreement figure by more than a point or two: the headline number stays healthy while the theme quietly disappears from your chart.

The full 200-row validation set is homework, and the cohort’s combined labels are pooled overnight so Friday morning has a real gold standard to argue about.

Stretch

Two options, pick one.

A · Run it locally. Install a local model, run the same classification, and compare the two on your validation set. Report the accuracy gap and the time cost. This is the number your risk function will want, and almost nobody has measured it.

B · Severity, and the money. Ask the classifier to score severity 1–5 and to extract the specific product mentioned. Join to the customer table. Now compute the value at risk behind each theme — not how many people complained about billing, but how much annual contribution sits behind those complaints.

Option B turns a theme chart into a prioritised remediation budget, and it is the version that gets funded — a chart of complaint volumes invites a discussion about whether the complaints matter, while a ranked list of contribution at risk invites a decision about which to fix first.

Copilot check

The failure mode here is not the usual one. The assistant is not wrong and it is not hallucinating. It is agreeable, and agreeableness is much harder to notice.

Ask it to classify reviews into your seven categories and it will classify every single review into one of your seven categories — including the reviews that belong in none of them. Somebody typing “test” to see whether the form works. A review left for a different company. A three-word fragment that could mean anything. Each comes back with a confident label and no note explaining that the category was a poor fit, because the taxonomy you supplied is treated as complete on the strength of your having supplied it.

Two fixes, both cheap:

  1. Always include an other and a not_applicable category, and monitor their share. The share is itself the instrument. A small and stable share is fine; a share that climbs from one quarter to the next is your early warning that reality has moved — a new product, a new failure, a new kind of customer — and your taxonomy has not. Without those categories the signal has nowhere to appear, and the change shows up instead as a quiet distortion spread across the seven you did define.
  2. Ask it to flag its own uncertainty. Add a confidence field to the schema, then hand-review the low-confidence rows. Treat that figure as a rough sort rather than a probability — it is not calibrated and should not be reported — but as a way of choosing which rows deserve a human’s forty minutes it works well, because taxonomy problems live disproportionately in that group.

Takeaways

  1. A classifier is a measurement instrument. Two hundred hand-labelled rows, or you have no error rate.
  2. Read the confusion matrix. Disagreement clusters reveal taxonomy problems, not model problems.
  3. Constrain the output, cache it, seed it, pin the model version.
  4. Always have an other category and watch its share.
  5. Where it runs is a legal question. There is a local option, and it is usually good enough.
