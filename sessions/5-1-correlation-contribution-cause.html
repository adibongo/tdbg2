Correlation, contribution, cause

Three claims, three evidence standards — Session 5.1, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can distinguish three fundamentally different claims that get made from the same chart, say which evidence standard each requires, and — most importantly — explain why a SHAP importance chart is not a list of things to fix.

Three claims, and only one of them justifies spending money

Three quite different statements can be made about the same pair of columns, and in a slide they look almost identical. It is worth separating them slowly, because the whole of Friday morning rests on the distinction.

The first is a statement about what tends to happen together. Customers who use the app a lot tend to stay; customers who use it rarely tend to leave. That is an observation about a pattern in data you already have, and it can be checked by anybody with the same file.

The second is a statement about what a model did. A model has scored a particular customer as high-risk, and you can take that single score apart and say which pieces of information pushed it up and which pushed it down. This is a fact about the model’s arithmetic, not about the customer’s life.

The third is a statement about what would happen if you intervened. If we persuaded this customer to use the app more, would they be less likely to leave? That is a claim about a world that does not yet exist, and nothing in a historical dataset can settle it on its own.

Those three are correlation, contribution and cause. Only the third justifies spending money on change, and only the third requires evidence you cannot get by looking harder at the data you already have.

Claim What it says Evidence needed What you may do with it
Correlation X and Y move together Observational data Predict. Target. Segment.
Contribution X pushed this prediction up A model, plus SHAP or similar Explain a model’s output. Debug a model.
Cause Changing X changes Y Experiment, or a strong quasi-experimental design Decide to spend money

A great many analytics presentations make claim three using evidence for claim one, and the room rarely notices, because nothing announces the change. The chart does not move; only the verb does. “Digital engagement drives retention” is a causal sentence resting on a correlational chart, and the slide before it said “is associated with”. That is the whole mechanism by which a bank comes to spend ₦400m pushing customers into an app in the hope of retaining them — not through anybody’s dishonesty, but through a series of small, natural paraphrases, each of which sounds like the last.

The sentence discipline

Watch the verbs. Each of these makes a different claim, and only some of them are usually supported by what is on the page:

  • “is associated with” — correlation. Honest.
  • “predicts” — correlation, used for targeting. Honest.
  • “contributes to the model’s prediction” — contribution. Honest and narrow.
  • “drives”, “causes”, “leads to”, “improves” — causal. Needs an experiment.

Almost the whole session is contained in that list, and it makes a practical test you can apply to somebody else’s memo in thirty seconds as well as to your own. If a sentence uses a verb from the last line, either the design that supports it is described somewhere in the document, or the verb needs to change. Both are acceptable outcomes. Only leaving it unexamined is not.

The classic confounder, using the marketing data

library(tidyverse)
mkt <- read_csv("data/marketing_spend.csv")

mkt |>
  group_by(channel) |>
  summarise(cor = cor(spend_ngn, conversions),
            cost_per_conversion = sum(spend_ngn) / sum(conversions))

Run that and every channel shows a strong positive correlation between what was spent and how many conversions arrived. Weeks with heavy spend are weeks with many conversions, in search, in display, in every line of the file. The obvious conclusion, and the one that will be reached in the meeting if nobody intervenes, is: spend more.

Before reading on, it is worth asking what else could produce that pattern.

Why that is not what the data says

Spend and conversions both respond to seasonal demand. When demand is high, the marketing team spends more and more people convert — and neither of those facts caused the other.

# The confounder is visible the moment you look
mkt |>
  mutate(week = lubridate::week(week_starting)) |>
  group_by(week) |>
  summarise(spend = sum(spend_ngn), conv = sum(conversions)) |>
  ggplot(aes(week)) +
  geom_line(aes(y = scale(spend)), colour = "#0B5E82") +
  geom_line(aes(y = scale(conv)),  colour = "#D99A2B") +
  labs(title = "Spend and conversions move together — because demand moves both")

A variable that causes both of the things you are comparing is called a confounder, and seasonal demand is the textbook example: it raises conversions directly, and it raises spend because the marketing team quite sensibly puts money behind the periods when people are buying. The correlation between spend and conversions is then perfectly real and almost entirely uninformative about the next naira.

Worse, the genuine response to spend diminishes as you spend more: doubling the budget does not double conversions, because the easiest customers are reached first. So the naive correlation overstates the return on the next naira in two directions at once — it credits spend with demand it did not create, and it assumes the next naira works as well as the average one.

The chain that makes this expensive:

  1. Correlation observed: high spend weeks have high conversions.
  2. Causal language used: “search drives conversions.”
  3. Budget decision made: increase search spend 40%.
  4. Conversions rise — because it was Q4.
  5. The decision is validated by the outcome, and repeated next year.

Nothing in that chain is stupid. Everything in it is wrong. Notice in particular that step 4 supplies apparently confirming evidence, which is what makes the loop self-sustaining: the result you predicted did arrive, so the belief is strengthened, and the same decision is made with more confidence next year. Only step 5 is preventable, and only by having designed a comparison at step 3 — holding some of the budget flat somewhere, so that “what would have happened anyway” is a thing you measured rather than a thing you assumed.

What SHAP actually is

This is the part of the day that most changes what people do on Monday, and it is worth building up from the plain idea before the acronym arrives.

Suppose a model gives a particular customer a churn score. You would like to know why it gave that customer that score. The natural way to find out is to ask how the score would change if one piece of information about them were different — if their app usage were typical instead of very low, say — and to do that for every piece of information the model used, in a way that makes the pieces add up to the score. That is exactly what SHAP values are: a decomposition of one single prediction into a contribution from each feature. The name comes from Shapley values, an idea borrowed from co-operative game theory about how to divide credit fairly among players who contributed jointly to a result.

Take a real row from the file in front of you. Customer C100306 scores 57% against a base rate of 10.6% — a little over five times the average risk in the book. Decomposing that prediction, the largest single push comes from digital_engagement_score = 9.9 — near the floor of the distribution — the next from products_held = 1, then tenure_months = 34 and a low balance. In plain terms: this customer barely uses the digital channel, holds a single product, is a few years in, and does not keep much money with the bank.

(Those four are the largest contributions from a logistic model fitted on the clean columns. Run it yourself; the numbers are in the file, not in this page.)

One technical caveat is worth carrying, because it is the most common way this gets misquoted. The contributions add up in whatever space the model does its arithmetic in, and for a classifier that is normally log-odds rather than probability. Log-odds is simply a rescaling of probability that runs from minus infinity to plus infinity instead of from 0 to 1, which is what lets the model add things up without ever producing a probability of 120%. The consequence for you is practical: a contribution of a given size in log-odds does not translate into a fixed number of percentage points, because the same push matters much more near a probability of 50% than near 2%. Attributing “six percentage points” to a feature requires an explainer configured for probability output, and even then the decomposition stops being cleanly additive. Unless you have done that work, say “the largest contribution” and not “worth six points”.

So SHAP is a genuinely useful and precisely defined thing. It answers: why did the model say this about this customer? — and that is a question worth being able to answer, both for debugging a model and for giving a retention caller something concrete to open with.

It does not answer: what should we change?

Say this out loud once and it will stay with you

A SHAP value is a contribution to a prediction, not a causal driver.

It tells you what the model used. It does not tell you what the world does.

The distinction is easy to state and genuinely hard to hold on to under the pressure of a slide titled “top drivers of churn”, so here are the three ways the misreading goes wrong in practice. Each has cost somebody real money.

One — the feature is a symptom. complaints_last_12m contributes heavily to the churn prediction, because people who are about to leave complain first. Read causally, that says “reduce complaints and churn will fall”. But the cheapest way to reduce the feature is to make complaints harder to log, and a team measured on it will find that route without anybody intending fraud. Complaints would fall, churn would not, the model would quietly get worse because its best signal had been switched off, and nobody would be able to explain why.

Two — correlated features share credit arbitrarily. txns_last_90d and digital_engagement_score measure nearly the same underlying thing: how active this customer is. When two features carry the same information, the model can lean on either, and SHAP has to split the credit between them somehow. How it splits depends on the fitting procedure rather than on the world. Refit with a different algorithm and the two can swap places in the ranking while the model’s predictions barely change. A “top drivers” chart that reorders when you change the model is not describing reality; it is describing the model, and it is worth checking whether it does, which is one of the lab tasks below.

Three — the intervention is not in the data. This is the deepest of the three. The model has never seen a customer whose engagement was changed by anything. It has only seen customers who differ from one another — and a customer who was always highly engaged is not the same kind of person as one who was pushed into the app by a campaign. Those are two different populations, and the difference between them is precisely the causal question. No amount of historical data answers it, because the observation that would answer it was never made.

What you may legitimately say

The gap between these two paragraphs is narrower than it looks and matters more than almost anything else on the page.

Legitimate:

“The model relies most heavily on complaints, digital engagement and tenure. Customers flagged high-risk typically show a recent complaint and falling app usage. This gives us a targeting rule and a conversation opener for the retention call.”

Not legitimate:

“Complaints are the biggest driver of churn. Reducing complaints by 20% would cut churn by 8%.”

The first describes who to call and what to raise with them, which is worth real money and is fully supported by what you have. The second promises a return on an intervention: it will be put in a business case, somebody will be given a budget and a target against it, and the only kind of evidence that supports it is an experiment. Read the two again and notice how little separates them on the page — one is a statement about the model’s inputs, the other about the world’s mechanics — and how completely they differ in what they license.

The bridge to causal work

If you want the causal claim, there are three routes, in descending order of strength.

  1. Randomise. 5.2. The strongest, and usually cheaper than people expect, because the comparison group costs nothing but the discipline of holding it back.
  2. Find a natural experiment. 5.3. Weaker, and sometimes the only route available, because the change already happened and nobody was going to randomise a regulation.
  3. Assume a causal structure and defend it. Write down the causal diagram you are assuming, state what you have adjusted for, and be explicit that the whole claim rests on there being no important confounder you failed to measure. This is a legitimate scientific move, used routinely in epidemiology and economics, and it is far more honest than a SHAP chart presented as a list of drivers. What it costs you is exposure: you have to put the diagram on the wall and let people attack it, and some of the attacks will land.

The causal diagram, in ninety seconds

Before any causal claim, sketch the boxes and arrows. It takes a minute on a whiteboard and it is the cheapest way to find out whether your analysis is answering the question you think it is. There are four kinds of thing to draw, and the difference between them decides what you should and should not control for.

  • Treatment → Outcome. The effect you are claiming. One arrow.
  • Confounders → both. Anything that causes the treatment and the outcome, like the seasonal demand above. These must be adjusted for, because otherwise their influence is credited to your treatment.
  • Mediators. Things that sit on the path between treatment and outcome — part of how the effect happens. These must not be adjusted for. Control for a mediator and you erase the very effect you are trying to measure, because you have held constant the mechanism through which it works.
  • Colliders. Things that treatment and outcome both cause. Conditioning on one of these manufactures an association out of nothing, and it produces the most spectacular spurious findings in the literature.

The collider is the one nobody draws, so it is worth taking slowly, and you have already met a live example. On Wednesday, sales_touchpoints was recorded only for leads that reached SQL — and reaching SQL is caused by two separate things: how hard the sales team worked the lead, and how good the lead was to begin with. A thing with two causes pointing into it is a collider. Filtering your analysis to the rows where touchpoints are populated is conditioning on that collider, and here is why it bites: among leads that got through, a lead that was worked lightly must have been strong, and a weak lead must have been worked hard, because otherwise it would not be in the file at all. Effort and quality now appear negatively related, and that relationship was created by the filter.

Note precisely how strong that claim is, because it is stronger than the one people usually make. “Survivorship” says your sample is unrepresentative of the population. A collider says the correlation itself was manufactured by the act of filtering — it was not there, you made it, and collecting more data on the surviving rows will not make it go away.

The mediator error is the more common one in commercial work, and it looks completely reasonable while you are making it: measuring the effect of a retention call on churn while controlling for “customer contacted us afterwards”. Being contacted afterwards is part of how the call works, so controlling for it subtracts the effect from itself.

Draw it on the whiteboard. If nobody can attack your diagram, either it is right or nobody understood it, and it is worth finding out which.

Core lab · 30 minutes

  • Take the marketing data. Compute the naive spend-conversion correlation by channel.
  • Show that seasonality drives both. Quantify how much of the correlation survives once you condition on the season.
  • Fit the churn model from 3.5 and compute SHAP values.
  • Write two sentences from the same chart: one legitimate targeting claim, one illegitimate causal claim.
  • Refit with a different algorithm. Does the “top drivers” ranking change? Report by how much.
  • Draw the causal diagram for your capstone’s central claim. Name the confounder you cannot measure.

The last item is the one to protect if the clock beats you. Every observational causal claim has a confounder somebody could name, and naming yours before the panel does is the difference between a limitation you have accounted for and one you have been caught by.

Stretch

Go back through your capstone brief and audit every verb.

Circle every sentence that makes a causal claim. For each one, write down what evidence you actually have, and then either downgrade the verb to match the evidence or specify the design that would support the verb you wanted.

Most people find between two and five, and the discovery is nearly always the same: the causal verbs crept in during the rewrite that made the memo read well, not during the analysis. It is the most uncomfortable twenty minutes of the programme, and it is the one that most improves the Friday defence — because the panel will do this to you anyway, and it is a great deal better to have done it to yourself first.

Copilot check

Assistants use causal language freely, because the writing they learned from does. Ask why churn is happening and you will get “the main drivers are…” — a causal framing built on correlational evidence, delivered fluently and with no signal at all that a line has been crossed. It is not being careless; it is reproducing the register of the millions of business documents that make exactly the same move.

They are, however, good at catching it when asked directly, which is the useful asymmetry here:

Here is a paragraph from an analytics memo. For each sentence, classify the claim as correlational, contributional or causal. For every causal claim, state what study design would be required to support it, and rewrite the sentence to match the evidence I actually have, which is: [describe your data].

Run your capstone through that prompt tonight. It is the cheapest possible rehearsal for the panel, and the rewritten sentences are usually good enough to keep.

Takeaways

  1. Three claims, three evidence standards. Only causal claims justify spending money on change.
  2. Watch the verbs. “Drives”, “causes” and “improves” need an experiment.
  3. A SHAP value is a contribution to a prediction, not a causal driver.
  4. Correlated features share SHAP credit arbitrarily. If the ranking moves when the model changes, it is not describing the world.
  5. If you must make a causal claim from observational data, draw the diagram and let people attack it.
