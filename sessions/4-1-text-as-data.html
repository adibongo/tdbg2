Text as data

Five thousand reviews into themes — and calibrating the classifier that labels them — Session 4.1, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can turn several thousand free-text customer comments into a small number of themes with volumes attached, decide sensibly between a word-counting approach and a language model, and — the part that makes the difference — establish whether the labels you have been handed are good enough to act on. A classifier is a measurement instrument, and an instrument nobody has calibrated is decoration.

Most of what you know about your customers is text

Some of what your organisation knows about its customers sits in structured tables. A much larger share of it is in sentences somebody typed: call-centre notes, the free-text box at the bottom of a branch survey, app store reviews, WhatsApp complaints, relationship manager comments, exit interview transcripts, supplier emails, and the “any other comments” field that has been quietly filling up for years and has never been read end to end.

For most of the history of corporate analytics, reading all of that was economically impossible — five thousand reviews at two minutes each is more than a fortnight of somebody’s working life — so the sensible thing was to keep the part that was already a number and let the words go. That was a reasonable trade at the time, and it is worth being precise about what it cost. A star rating tells you that somebody is unhappy, and roughly how unhappy. The words tell you what to fix. Only one of those two can be turned into a work item on a Monday morning.

The cost of reading text at scale has now collapsed. What has not changed is that the answer you get out is only as good as the corpus you were handed and the care you took over the counting — and the last section of this session, about who filtered the file before it reached you, matters more than everything before it.

The classical pipeline still matters

Before reaching for a language model, it is worth running the version of this analysis that has existed for thirty years. It is not a courtesy to tradition. It costs nothing, it runs in seconds on a laptop, every step of it can be explained to an auditor line by line, and it answers the question outright more often than people expect.

The pipeline has four steps, and each has a plain meaning underneath a technical name.

Tokenise → remove stop words → count → compare.

To tokenise is to cut the text into individual words, so that a review becomes a list rather than a sentence; each word is then called a token. Stop words are the words that appear in nearly every sentence and so distinguish nothing between them — “the”, “and”, “is”, “of” — and the conventional list of them is called a stop-word list. What is left is a table you can count.

  • R (tidytext)
  • Python
library(tidyverse)
library(tidytext)

reviews <- read_csv("data/customer_reviews.csv")

words <- reviews |>
  unnest_tokens(word, review_text) |>          # one row per word
  anti_join(stop_words, by = "word") |>        # drop "the", "and", "is"
  filter(!str_detect(word, "^[0-9]+$"))        # drop bare numbers

words |> count(word, sort = TRUE) |> head(20)

Both versions do the same three things — one row per word, drop the words that carry no signal, count what remains — and both give you a first frequency table.

The move that turns word counts into a finding

That first table is nearly always a disappointment, and it is worth expecting that rather than concluding you have done something wrong. “Account”, “app” and “bank” will sit at the top of it — as they will for every bank, in every market, in every quarter. Frequency in a single corpus mostly measures what the corpus is about, which you already knew.

The move that rescues the analysis is to stop counting one pile of text and start comparing two. Three comparisons do most of the work:

  • the words that appear in one-star reviews and not in five-star reviews;
  • the words that were rare last quarter and are common now;
  • the words that appear on WhatsApp and never appear in the branch survey.

Each turns a description into a difference, and a difference is something somebody can act on. Here is the first:

library(tidylo)

# What distinguishes detractors from promoters?
words |>
  filter(star_rating != 3) |>                                    # drop the middle FIRST
  mutate(sentiment_group = if_else(star_rating <= 2,
                                   "detractor", "promoter")) |>
  count(sentiment_group, word) |>
  bind_log_odds(sentiment_group, word, n) |>                     # weighted, prior-smoothed
  filter(sentiment_group == "detractor", n > 25) |>
  slice_max(log_odds_weighted, n = 15)

Read that block in English. Set the three-star reviews aside, because they are neither a complaint nor a compliment and including them only blurs the contrast. Split what remains into detractors and promoters, count how often each group uses each word, then score every word by how much more characteristic it is of detractors — taking the fifteen highest, restricted to words used at least twenty-five times so the list is not dominated by one person’s unusual vocabulary.

Frequency describes the corpus. Difference describes the problem. That one substitution is most of the value in classical text analysis.

Do not compute the raw log-odds by hand

The obvious way to score a word is to divide its detractor count by its promoter count and take the logarithm — in code, log((detractor + 1) / (promoter + 1)). That is a perfectly natural thing to write, and it is biased in a direction that is easy to miss.

The arithmetic shows why. Detractors are 26% of this corpus and promoters are 60%, so there are roughly 60 ÷ 26 ≈ 2.3 promoter reviews for every detractor review. A word used at exactly the same rate by both groups will therefore still turn up about 2.3 times more often in the promoter pile, purely because that pile is bigger. Rank by the raw ratio and you are ranking group size as much as meaning.

bind_log_odds() implements the weighted log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior (Monroe, Colaresi and Quinn, 2008). The log-odds ratio part asks how much more likely a word is in one group than the other, on a scale where “twice as likely” and “half as likely” sit the same distance either side of zero. The informative prior part pulls the estimate for a rare word back towards what the corpus as a whole does, so a word seen four times cannot leap over a word seen four hundred times on the strength of a coincidence. Together they normalise for group size and keep noise off the top of the list.

One function instead of one arithmetic expression, and the ranking becomes about the words rather than about the sample.

Bigrams, because single words lie

Counting words one at a time throws away the order they came in, and order is where much of the meaning of a complaint lives. “Not working” and “working” contain the same word, and once the sentence has been cut into tokens there is nothing left to tell them apart. Since negation is how people express most complaints — the thing that did not happen, the money that was not returned — this is not a minor loss.

The fix is to count pairs of adjacent words. A pair like that is called a bigram; three together is a trigram, and the general term is an n-gram.

bigrams <- reviews |>
  unnest_tokens(bigram, review_text, token = "ngrams", n = 2) |>
  separate(bigram, c("w1", "w2"), sep = " ") |>
  filter(!w1 %in% stop_words$word | w1 %in% c("not", "no", "never"),
         !w2 %in% stop_words$word)

bigrams |> count(w1, w2, sort = TRUE) |> head(20)

The filter() line repays a slow read. It keeps a bigram if the first word is not a stop word — or if it is one of “not”, “no” or “never”. That exception exists because standard stop-word lists classify all three as noise and delete them. “Not reversed”, “no response”, “never resolved”: those are the phrases that carry the complaint, and the default configuration removes every one of them before you get to see it.

Why the word cloud is usually the wrong deliverable

Word clouds are popular for understandable reasons. They are immediate, they photograph well on a slide, and they make it obvious that a lot of text was involved. None of those are bad things to want from a chart.

The difficulty is what a word cloud does with the numbers it was given. It encodes quantity as font size, and people compare type sizes poorly — far less accurately than they compare lengths along a common baseline, which is what a bar chart offers. It encodes position as nothing at all, so the most readable channel on the page is spent packing words into a pleasing shape. Long words look larger than short ones at identical frequency, and with no axis, scale or ordering a reader cannot tell whether “delayed” appeared 400 times or 40.

Two settings do earn one: as a first look for yourself, when you have never seen a corpus, and as decoration on a slide whose point is “there is a great deal of text here”. Neither is a decision paper.

What to send instead: the five themes, their volumes, their trend, and one verbatim quote each. That is a business case — it names what is wrong, says how much of it there is, says whether it is getting worse, and lets a reader hear a customer say it in their own words. A word cloud is a picture.

The right-hand panel is doing something honest and slightly uncomfortable. Each theme’s share moved between quarters, and each movement carries an interval showing the range of values consistent with the data. Every one of those intervals includes zero — meaning that for every theme, “no change at all” remains a perfectly good explanation. The volumes on the left are solid enough to plan against; the movements on the right are not yet distinguishable from noise, and presenting them as a trend is the most common way a text analysis loses credibility on the second reading.

The multilingual problem, which is not optional here

About one review in twelve in this corpus — 8.6% — is in Nigerian Pidgin. On 4,938 reviews that is roughly 425 of them, which is more than enough to move a theme ranking.

"dem debit me two times, e never reverse"
"network no dey work for my area"
"customer care no pick my call at all"

An English stop-word list does nothing useful with these sentences: it does not recognise “dey” or “dem” as function words, so they survive into the frequency table as though they were content. An English sentiment lexicon — a dictionary that assigns a positive or negative score to each word — reads “e never reverse”, finds no word it holds an opinion about, and scores a clear complaint as neutral. The same happens with code-switching, where a writer moves between languages inside a single sentence, which is entirely normal in everyday Nigerian writing and completely outside what a single-language lexicon is built for.

The consequence is not random error, and that is what makes it serious. A classical pipeline systematically under-counts complaints from the segment most likely to write in Pidgin — and in most Nigerian consumer businesses that is not a random slice of the customer base. The themes that group cares about are quietly shifted down the ranking, and nothing in the output signals that it happened.

This is a real, quantifiable bias with a commercial consequence, and it is the strongest argument for the language-model approach in 4.1 — which handles Pidgin, code-switching and mid-sentence language changes without any additional work.

Whichever route you take, note it in your method section. “9% of the corpus is in Nigerian Pidgin and was handled by the classifier rather than by the lexicon” is the kind of sentence that makes a technical reviewer trust the rest of your paper, because it shows you knew the limitation was there rather than hoping nobody would ask.

The filter

Now the part that matters more than any of the above, and the part that no amount of skill with the previous sections will save you from.

reviews |> summarise(n = n(),
                     mean_stars = mean(star_rating),
                     detractors = mean(star_rating <= 2))
#> 4,938   3.52   0.260

A mean of 3.52 stars, 26% detractors. Both figures are arithmetically correct, and both describe a population somebody else defined before the file reached you. That is not a criticism of whoever produced the extract: extracts are always scoped, usually for a good operational reason, and the person scoping one is answering the question they were asked rather than the question you are about to ask. The habit worth building is to look for the scope before quoting the numbers.

Look at the column nobody looks at

customer_still_active.

Cross-tabulate it against the star rating:

SELECT customer_still_active, star_rating, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM 'data/customer_reviews.csv'
GROUP BY 1, 2 ORDER BY 1, 2;

Among customers who are still active, one- and two-star reviews are 30% of the file. Among customers who have left, they are 8%. Side by side, that is odd: the departed group looks close to four times less likely to be angry than the group who stayed, when the expectation runs firmly the other way. Sixty-eight such reviews survive where several hundred should be.

Somebody built a “voice of the active customer” report and filtered out most low-star reviews from customers who had already gone — a defensible thing to do for that report’s purpose. Carried into this analysis, it means sentiment reads several points better than reality, the top complaint themes are under-weighted exactly where they were most severe, and every conclusion drawn from this file is a conclusion about the people who stayed.

And you were lucky

Notice what made that cross-tabulation possible. The column the filter was built on was left in the export. A tidier extract would have dropped it — the report only needed active customers, so the flag was redundant — and there would then have been nothing in the file to compare against. No data quality rule would have caught it, because every row present is valid. No amount of reading the review text would have caught it, because the missing reviews are missing. Next quarter’s extract may well be tidier.

So the defence cannot be analytical. It is procedural, and it fits in one sentence:

Ask for the extraction query, not the extract.

Then read the WHERE clause. That takes about ten seconds, it requires no tooling, and it is the single most valuable habit on Day 4.

The most dangerous sentence in an analytics review

“Here is what the data says.”

The honest version is “here is what the data that reached me says”, and the gap between the two is where most avoidable analytical embarrassment lives. It is not a gap about skill: somebody can run every technique in this session flawlessly and still be describing a population they never chose.

Every extract has a WHERE clause. Somebody chose it, usually for a sound operational reason that had nothing to do with your question, and usually months before your question existed. Asking what it was costs one email and is never resented.

From counting words to reading them

Everything so far has counted words. That is genuinely useful, it is fast, and it is completely transparent: when a lexicon assigns a review to a theme you can see exactly which word did it. It also has a ceiling, and we have just walked into it. Sarcasm, negation, code-switching and the ordinary compression of real speech all defeat a method whose entire model of language is a list.

The alternative is to hand the text to a model that has some representation of meaning, and ask it for a label. This works considerably better, and it introduces a problem the lexicon never had. A word list that does not recognise a review returns nothing, and the gap is visible in your output. A language model asked to pick one of seven categories will pick one. Every time. For every row. Including the rows it has no business having an opinion about.

That is the trade this half of the session is about: better labels, and a failure mode that no longer announces itself.

What the model can and cannot be trusted to do

Before writing any code it is worth being precise about the shape of this tool, because its strengths and weaknesses are not quite where intuition puts them.

It is reliable at recognition. Given a review and a fixed list of categories, deciding which one the review is about is close to what these models do natively. It reads “transfer failed but they debited me” and returns billing for much the same reason a person would — in Pidgin, in English, and in a sentence that switches between the two halfway through. It is untroubled by misspellings, missing punctuation and the compressed register people use on a phone.

It is unreliable at anything requiring knowledge it was not given. It does not know your product names, your internal process boundaries, or that “the Tuesday run” means the replenishment cycle. If the distinction between two of your categories depends on institutional knowledge, the model cannot make it — and it will not tell you that it could not.

It is weakest where humans are also weak, but it fails differently. Sarcasm, irony and mixed sentiment are genuinely hard, and two careful human labellers will disagree on them too. The difference is that a human labeller says “I am not sure about this one”. The model does not.

The failure mode to design against is the confident wrong label

Consider what a classical lexicon does with a sentence it does not understand. It finds no scoring word, returns neutral or nothing, and the row shows up as unclassified. The gap is visible; you can count it, and its size tells you how far to trust the rest.

A language model given an enum of seven categories returns one of those seven for every row it is shown, whatever the row contains. There is no “I do not know” in the schema unless you put one there. A review it has misread therefore does not arrive marked as doubtful — it arrives as branch_service, in the same column, indistinguishable from the rows it got right. The errors are silent, and silent errors do not get investigated.

Two further properties make this worse than it first looks:

  1. The errors are not random, so they do not average out. If the classifier is weaker on Pidgin than on English, every theme those customers disproportionately raise is understated — consistently, in the same direction, every quarter. Random error shrinks as the corpus grows. Systematic error does not shrink at all; a bigger corpus simply gives you a more precise estimate of the wrong number.
  2. Nothing downstream can recover it. Once the label is in the table, every chart and trend built on top inherits it, and each step makes the number look more solid. By the time it reaches a slide it has acquired a decimal place and a comparison against last quarter.

None of this argues against using the tool. It argues for the one countermeasure that works: hold some rows out and check them by hand. No diagnostic computed from the model’s own output, and no self-assessment you can ask it for, substitutes for a sample somebody labelled independently.

Classifying the corpus

  • R (ellmer — any provider)

mall applies a local language model to a data frame column, row by row. Nothing leaves the machine, which for confidential text is frequently the difference between doing the work and not doing it.

library(tidyverse)
library(mall)

# Point at a locally-running model. No data leaves this laptop.
llm_use("ollama", "llama3.2", seed = 42, .cache = "_llm_cache")

reviews <- read_csv("data/customer_reviews.csv")

labelled <- reviews |>
  llm_sentiment(review_text, options = c("positive", "neutral", "negative")) |>
  llm_classify(review_text,
               labels = c("billing", "app_reliability", "network_quality",
                          "branch_service", "delivery", "pricing", "other"),
               pred_name = "theme") |>
  llm_extract(review_text,
              labels = "the single product or service the customer is talking about",
              pred_name = "subject")

labelled |> count(theme, .sentiment) |> pivot_wider(names_from = .sentiment, values_from = n)
library(ellmer)

chat <- chat_anthropic(system_prompt = paste(
  "You classify customer reviews for a Nigerian bank.",
  "Reviews may be in English or Nigerian Pidgin.",
  "Return only the requested structured fields."
))

schema <- type_object(
  theme     = type_enum(values = c("billing", "app_reliability", "network_quality",
                                   "branch_service", "delivery", "pricing", "other")),
  sentiment = type_enum(values = c("positive", "neutral", "negative")),
  intent    = type_enum(values = c("complaint", "praise", "question",
                                   "churn_threat", "other")),
  severity  = type_integer(description = "1 = trivial, 5 = would leave over this")
)

classify_one <- function(txt) chat$chat_structured(txt, type = schema)

# Batch, cache, and retry. Do not run 5,000 rows in a naive loop.
labelled <- reviews |>
  mutate(result = map(review_text, possibly(classify_one, otherwise = NULL))) |>
  unnest_wider(result)
import pandas as pd, json, time, pathlib, anthropic
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
SCHEMA = {"theme": ["billing","app_reliability","network_quality","branch_service",
                    "delivery","pricing","other"],
          "sentiment": ["positive","neutral","negative"],
          "intent": ["complaint","praise","question","churn_threat","other"]}

PROMPT = (
    "Classify this customer review for a Nigerian bank. It may be in English or "
    "Nigerian Pidgin. Return ONLY JSON with keys theme, sentiment, intent, severity "
    "(1-5). Allowed values: " + json.dumps(SCHEMA) + "\n\nReview: "
)

MODEL = "claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929"      # PIN the dated version, not the alias


def _extract_json(txt):
    """Models add prose. Take the JSON object and nothing else."""
    i, j = txt.find("{"), txt.rfind("}")
    if i == -1 or j == -1:
        raise ValueError(f"no JSON in response: {txt[:80]}")
    return json.loads(txt[i:j + 1])


def classify(text, tries=4):
    for k in range(tries):
        try:
            r = client.messages.create(
                model=MODEL, max_tokens=150, temperature=0,   # temperature=0
                messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT + text}])
            return _extract_json(r.content[0].text)
        except Exception:
            if k == tries - 1:
                return {"theme": "other", "sentiment": "neutral",
                        "intent": "other", "error": True}
            time.sleep(2 ** k)                                 # backoff


reviews = pd.read_csv("data/customer_reviews.csv")
out, path = [], pathlib.Path("outputs/labels.jsonl")
done = {json.loads(l)["review_id"] for l in path.open()} if path.exists() else set()
with path.open("a") as fh, ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=6) as pool:
    todo = reviews[~reviews.review_id.isin(done)]              # resume, don't restart
    for rid, res in zip(todo.review_id, pool.map(classify, todo.review_text)):
        fh.write(json.dumps({"review_id": rid, **res}) + "\n")  # write incrementally

All three do the same thing — send each review to a model with a fixed list of permitted answers, and write the answer back as a column. They differ only in where the model runs and how much of the machinery you build yourself.

Four operational things that will bite you

Cache. You will re-run this — to fix a bug, to add a category, to answer a question somebody asked in a meeting. Without a cache you pay twice and, worse, you get slightly different answers the second time, and then you cannot reproduce your own chart. Cache on a hash of the input text, so identical text always yields the stored label.

Constrain the output. Use a schema or an enum — a list of permitted values the model must choose from. Ask for a free-text category instead and you will receive billing, Billing, billing issue and payment/billing as four distinct categories, and spend an hour reconciling them into one.

Pin the model version and set temperature to zero. “We used the model” is not a method. “We used model X, dated version Y, at temperature 0” is. When the number moves next quarter you need to be able to say whether the business changed or the instrument did — and an alias like claude-sonnet-4-5 points at different weights over time, which is exactly the ambiguity you are trying to remove. Temperature is the setting that controls how much randomness goes into the model’s choice of words; at zero it makes the most likely choice every time.

Note that not every API offers a seed parameter, and temperature 0 is not a guarantee of determinism. The cache is your reproducibility mechanism, not the seed: hash the input text, store the label, and never re-classify a row you have already classified.

Batch and retry. Five thousand sequential calls will take an hour and fail somewhere in the middle. Parallelise modestly, retry with backoff, and write results incrementally — the .jsonl file in the Python tab exists so that an interrupted run resumes rather than restarts.

Validation — the forty minutes that make it real

This is the section that separates a measurement from an assertion, and it is the one most often skipped — usually because the results already look plausible and there is a meeting on Thursday.

The procedure has four steps. First, draw a random sample — two hundred rows, not twenty. There is arithmetic behind that number rather than tradition: if the classifier agrees with a human on roughly 88% of rows, two hundred rows will contain about 200 × 0.12 = 24 disagreements, which is enough to see a pattern in. Forty rows would yield about five, which tells you whether something is catastrophically wrong but not where the boundary problems are.

Second, label them by hand, blind. A human reads the review and assigns a category without seeing what the model said. This matters more than it sounds: shown the model’s answer first, a labeller agrees with it far more often than they otherwise would. The phenomenon is called anchoring, it is not a weakness of the individual, and the remedy is to strip the model’s column out of the file before anybody opens it.

Third, compare. Fourth — and this is the step that repays the effort — look at where the disagreements fall rather than only at how many there are.

set.seed(1)
gold <- labelled |>
  slice_sample(n = 200) |>
  select(review_id, review_text) |>
  mutate(human_theme = NA_character_, human_sentiment = NA_character_)

write_csv(gold, "outputs/validation_blank.csv")
# --- a human fills this in, without seeing the model's answer ---

gold <- read_csv("outputs/validation_filled.csv") |>
  left_join(labelled |> select(review_id, theme, .sentiment), by = "review_id")

# Overall agreement
gold |> summarise(theme_agreement     = mean(theme == human_theme),
                  sentiment_agreement = mean(.sentiment == human_sentiment))

# The confusion matrix is where the useful information is
gold |> count(human_theme, theme) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = theme, values_from = n, values_fill = 0)

The first summarise() produces raw agreement — the share of rows on which model and human chose the same label. It is the obvious number to report, and it is not sufficient alone.

Do not report raw agreement without a floor to compare it against. With seven categories and the largest holding 21% of the corpus, a classifier that ignored the text entirely and guessed the majority class every time would score 21% while knowing nothing at all — and on a more concentrated corpus, where one theme holds half the rows, that floor is far higher.

Cohen’s kappa does that comparison for you. It asks how much of the agreement you observed is more than chance would have produced, expressed as a share of how much room for improvement there was: κ = (observed − chance) ÷ (1 − chance). A kappa of 0 means the classifier is doing no better than guessing; 1 means perfect agreement. It is one line:

irr::kappa2(gold[, c("human_theme", "theme")])

The arithmetic is worth following once. Suppose observed agreement comes out at 0.88 and the agreement chance alone would produce, given how the seven categories are distributed, comes out at 0.20. Then κ = (0.88 − 0.20) ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 0.68 ÷ 0.80 = 0.85 — the classifier captured 85% of the improvement available over guessing. Quoting that alongside the raw 88% tells a reviewer you understood the difference.

Then read the confusion matrix, not the headline number. A confusion matrix is a table with human labels down the side and model labels across the top, so each cell counts the rows where the human said one thing and the model said another. 88% agreement sounds fine; the matrix answers the more useful question of where the 12% went.

The typical pattern in this corpus is that billing and app_reliability get confused, because “the app debited me twice” is genuinely both — a payment failure that happened inside the app. That is not a model failure. It is a taxonomy failure: your two categories overlap, the model is being asked to choose between them on a review that satisfies both, and two human labellers would split on it as well. The fix is not a better model. Either merge the themes or add a rule to the prompt saying which wins when both apply — and say in the method note which you did.

Carry the distinction beyond this example. Disagreements scattered evenly across the matrix suggest the classifier is struggling. Disagreements piled into one or two cells suggest your categories are, and that is a problem you fix with a definition rather than with technology.

What to put in the paper

Themes were assigned by [model, dated version] at temperature 0 against a fixed seven-category taxonomy; labels were cached, and the cache is included with this analysis. A random sample of 200 reviews, stratified by language, was independently hand-labelled without sight of the model’s answer. Model and human agreed on 88% of theme assignments (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0.85) and 94% of sentiment. Disagreements concentrated on the billing / app-reliability boundary, where the review describes a payment failure inside the app; these two themes are therefore reported together. 8.6% of the corpus is in Nigerian Pidgin, where agreement is lower than in English; the sample contains too few Pidgin rows to state a separate error rate with confidence, and a stratified follow-up is planned.

Four sentences, and each does one job: the first makes the run reproducible, the second establishes that the check was independent, the third states the error rate and where it concentrates, and the fourth admits a limitation the reader could otherwise have found for themselves — which is the sentence that earns the other three their credibility.

It converts “the model said” into a measurement with a stated error, and it is the difference between a technical reviewer trusting your paper and quietly discounting it.

Where it runs, and why that is a board-level question

There are three places this work can happen, and the choice between them is a legal question before it is a technical one.

Where the data goes Speed Quality Use when
Hosted model Leaves your network, often the country Fast Best The text is not personal or confidential
Local model Nowhere Slower Good, and improving fast Customer records, HR text, anything under the NDPA
Enterprise / in-region deployment Your tenancy Fast Best Your organisation has procured it and DPIA’d it

For 4,938 anonymised course reviews any of the three is fine, which is why you can run today’s lab without a conversation with anybody.

Real call-centre notes are a different matter. They contain names, account numbers and the details of individual complaints, and sending them to a model hosted outside the country is a cross-border personal data transfer under the NDPA 2023, which needs a lawful basis established before anybody runs the script rather than discovered afterwards. That is where a promising internal project most often stops, and it stops late, after the work has been done.

The practical answer that unblocks most teams is to run it locally. A local model on a decent laptop classifies a few thousand short texts in an hour or two, costs nothing per call, works without internet, and never raises the transfer question at all. It is somewhat less accurate — and for theme classification against a fixed taxonomy, the gap is usually smaller than the gap between two human labellers doing the same job. That is the comparison to make when somebody asks whether the local option is good enough: good enough compared to what?

We come back to this properly in 5.4.

Core lab · part one · 25 minutes

  • Tokenise the corpus. Count. Note how uninformative raw frequency is.
  • Build the detractor-versus-promoter log-odds table. Now you have a finding.
  • Do it again with bigrams, keeping negations. Compare what you learn.
  • Isolate the Pidgin reviews. Run your English pipeline on them. Quantify how badly it does.
  • Find the filter. Cross-tabulate the two columns that reveal it and quantify the distortion.
  • Then answer the harder question: if that column had not been left in by accident, how would you have found out? Write the sentence you would send to whoever produced the extract.
  • Write the one-sentence caveat that must accompany every number from this corpus.

The sixth item is the one to spend real time on. The first five teach a technique; that one teaches the habit that survives after you have forgotten the technique.

Core lab · part two · 25 minutes

A classification pass over 4,938 reviews has already been run and cached — it is data/customer_reviews_labelled.csv, with the model and temperature recorded in the file. Seventy-one people classifying five thousand rows each, simultaneously, on conference wifi, is an hour of waiting and nothing else. The lab is about validation.

  • Classify fifty rows yourself so you have run the mechanics once: enum, schema, temperature zero, cache.
  • Join the cached labels to the corpus. Produce the theme-by-quarter volume table. That table is the deliverable, not the classifier.
  • Hand-label forty rows as a table, blind — do not look at the model’s answer first. Stratify: make sure some are Pidgin.
  • Compute agreement, and then Cohen’s kappa, and build the confusion matrix.
  • Report per-class recall, not just the overall rate. Find the class the classifier is worst on, and ask what it costs you to miss it.
  • Find the category boundary causing most of the disagreement. Fix the taxonomy or the prompt, and say which.
  • Write the four-sentence method paragraph.

Per-class recall, in the fifth item, is the share of rows genuinely belonging to a category that the classifier actually assigned to it. It matters because a small theme can be missed almost entirely without moving the overall agreement figure by more than a point or two: the headline number stays healthy while the theme quietly disappears from your chart.

The full 200-row validation set is homework, and the cohort’s combined labels are pooled overnight so Friday morning has a real gold standard to argue about.

Stretch

Bring your own text — call notes, survey free-text, complaint logs, anything you can lawfully use — and run the same pipeline.

Then, before you look at any output, write down the WHERE clause you think was on the extract, and go and check with whoever produced it. Comparing your guess against the real clause is the exercise, and being wrong is the interesting outcome rather than the embarrassing one. In most cohorts at least one person finds that a population they have been reporting on for years is narrower than they believed.

Copilot check

Assistants write text-processing code excellently, and there is no reason to type the boilerplate above by hand. Two things are worth checking every time:

  1. The default stop-word list will delete your negations. Not because the assistant erred, but because it used the standard list, and the standard list treats “not”, “no”, “never” and “without” as noise. Say otherwise explicitly in the prompt.
  2. It will offer you a word cloud. That is the most common output associated with this task in everything it has read, so it is a reasonable prediction of what people usually want. Ask instead for “the terms most over-represented in one-star reviews relative to five-star reviews, with counts and a log-odds ratio.”

And the thing it cannot do, for the same reason it could not detect Wednesday’s leak: it cannot tell you what the extraction filter was. That information is not in the file, in the text, or anywhere the assistant can reach. Only a person can ask that question, of another person.

Takeaways

  • Most of what your customers tell you is text, and most of it is never read. A few thousand comments reduce to a handful of themes with volumes attached, and that is a reportable result rather than a word cloud.
  • Word-counting methods are fast, transparent and honest about what they cannot do. Their gaps are visible. Start here, and keep the result as a benchmark.
  • A language model labels everything, including what it should not. Its errors are silent, systematic and correlated with things you care about, such as the language the review was written in.
  • A classifier is a measurement instrument. Validate it against a human-labelled sample before you quote a number from it, look at the confusion matrix rather than the headline agreement, and say the agreement statistic out loud when you present the result.
  • Where the model runs is a board-level question, not an IT preference. If the text contains anything your organisation could not send to a third party, that decision is made on Friday, not by whoever wrote the script.
