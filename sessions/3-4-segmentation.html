Segmentation you can act on

From cluster to persona to campaign — Session 3.4, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can build a segmentation, decide how many segments there should be on commercial rather than statistical grounds, name and size each one, attach one action to each, and check whether it is stable enough to run a campaign against next quarter.

The test a segmentation has to pass

A segmentation earns its keep when you would do something different for each segment. That is the whole test, and it is worth applying before any code is written, because it is the only part of the exercise that cannot be recovered later.

It is a demanding test, and a great many carefully built corporate segmentations do not quite pass it. The usual shape is six groups with evocative names, a slide carrying six photographs of people holding phones, and — when you look at what happens next — a single treatment applied to all six. Nobody set out to produce that. It happens because naming and describing groups is intrinsically satisfying work, and deciding what to do about them involves a different set of people who were not in the room.

So write the sentence before you cluster anything:

“If a customer is in segment 3, we will ______ instead of ______, and we expect ______.”

Three blanks, and all three matter. The first says what changes; the second says what would otherwise have happened, which is what makes the change measurable; the third commits you to an expectation you can be wrong about in public.

If you cannot complete that sentence for every segment, what you have built is a description rather than a segmentation. Descriptions are genuinely useful — they help people think, they give a marketing team a shared vocabulary, and they are often the first step towards something actionable. They simply should not be priced, resourced or reported as though they were decisions.

Start with RFM. It is not a poor relation.

Before reaching for any machine learning, run recency, frequency, monetary — usually shortened to RFM. The idea is about as old as direct mail: sort your customers by how recently they bought, how often they buy, and how much they spend, and treat the resulting groups differently. It is roughly fifty years old, it takes one query, and in most businesses it explains the great majority of what a sophisticated clustering will eventually find.

-- RFM on the retail banking base, scored into deciles.
WITH base AS (
  SELECT customer_id,
         tenure_months,
         txns_last_90d                       AS frequency,
         avg_balance_ngn                     AS monetary,
         digital_engagement_score            AS engagement
  FROM 'data/bank_customers.csv'
)
SELECT customer_id,
       NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY frequency)  AS f_score,
       NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY monetary)   AS m_score,
       NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY engagement) AS e_score
FROM base;

NTILE(5) is doing the work here: it sorts everybody on a column and cuts the sorted list into five equal-sized buckets, so a score of 5 means “top fifth on this measure”. Three scores of five levels each gives 125 possible combinations, which is far more than any organisation can act on.

The move that makes RFM useful is collapsing those 125 cells into the handful that carry a decision. High value and engaged — protect them. High value and disengaged — call them, because that combination is what leaving looks like early. Low value and engaged — grow them; the relationship is there and the balance is not. Low value and disengaged — serve them digitally and cheaply.

Four groups, four actions, four owners, one query.

Run RFM first, always

RFM gives you a baseline that any machine-learned segmentation then has to beat — in exactly the same way that the naive forecast in 3.3 gives you a baseline that a forecasting model has to beat. Without a baseline, “the clustering found four segments” is an unfalsifiable claim.

It matters commercially because clustering algorithms are very good at finding the largest source of variation in a table, and in customer data that is usually value. So if your k-means solution comes back with groups amounting to “high value”, “medium value” and “low value”, the honest reading is that three weeks of work has reproduced a NTILE — which happens more often than the case studies suggest, and is much better discovered on day one than at the steering committee in week six.

k-means, and the four things that go wrong

k-means is the workhorse clustering method, and the intuition behind it is simpler than its reputation. You tell it how many groups you want — that is the k. It drops that many points at random into the space your customers occupy, assigns every customer to the nearest one, moves each point to the middle of the customers it just collected, and repeats until nothing moves.

Two consequences follow, and both matter later. The method has no opinion about whether k is a sensible number — ask for nine and you get nine. And everything depends on what “nearest” means, which depends entirely on the units your columns happen to be measured in.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidymodels)

bank <- read_csv("data/bank_customers.csv")

# 1. Choose variables that relate to the DECISION, not everything available.
seg_vars <- bank |>
  select(customer_id, tenure_months, products_held, avg_balance_ngn,
         digital_engagement_score, txns_last_90d, complaints_last_12m)

# 2. Balance is log-normal. Cluster the log, or balance dominates everything.
prep <- recipe(~ ., data = seg_vars) |>
  update_role(customer_id, new_role = "id") |>
  step_log(avg_balance_ngn, base = 10) |>
  step_normalize(all_numeric_predictors()) |>     # 3. Scale. Non-negotiable.
  prep()

X <- bake(prep, new_data = NULL) |> select(-customer_id)

# 4. Try several k and look at the elbow - then ignore it and use judgement.
set.seed(42)
kfits <- tibble(k = 2:9) |>
  mutate(fit = map(k, ~ kmeans(X, centers = .x, nstart = 25)),
         wss = map_dbl(fit, ~ .x$tot.withinss))

ggplot(kfits, aes(k, wss)) + geom_line() + geom_point() +
  labs(title = "Within-cluster sum of squares by k",
       subtitle = "There is rarely a clean elbow in real business data. Choose on grounds you can defend.")

final <- kmeans(X, centers = 4, nstart = 50)
bank$segment <- factor(final$cluster)

Two lines in that recipe are worth pausing on, because they are the ones most often left out. step_log() compresses a very skewed column so that the gap between ₦10m and ₦100m counts about the same as the gap between ₦100k and ₦1m. step_normalize() puts every column on a common scale by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, so that a column measured in millions and a column measured in units contribute comparably. The nstart = 50 simply runs the whole procedure fifty times from different random starting points and keeps the best result, which protects you from an unlucky start.

The four things that go wrong, in order of frequency

None of these is an exotic failure. All four are what happens when a competent person runs a clustering the way the documentation example runs one.

1 · Not scaling. avg_balance_ngn runs to eight figures; products_held runs from 1 to 8. Distance is computed by adding up differences across columns, so before standardising, a ₦2m difference in balance swamps a difference of every single product a customer could hold. The arithmetic is not wrong; it is simply answering a question about balance and calling the answer a segmentation. This is the most common error of the four, and it is dangerous precisely because the output looks entirely plausible — you get tidy groups, they differ from one another, and nothing on screen indicates that only one column was consulted.

2 · Not transforming skewed variables. Balance in a retail bank is log-normal: most customers cluster near the bottom and a small number sit a very long way out. Cluster the raw column and a handful of private banking customers will each be far enough from everyone else to claim a cluster of their own. You then get four segments, three of which contain almost nobody, and a fourth containing the entire business.

3 · Throwing in every available variable. It feels thorough, and it is the natural instinct when the data is already in front of you. But branch_state encoded as a number invents an ordering that does not exist, an identifier adds pure noise, and anything that is a consequence of the segment rather than a characteristic of it quietly builds the answer into the question. Choose variables that relate to the decision you intend to make, and be able to say why each one is there.

4 · Believing the elbow. The elbow method plots how much unexplained variation remains as k increases, and looks for the bend where adding another group stops helping much. In textbook data there is a clear bend. In real business data the line declines smoothly and there is nothing that anybody would honestly call an elbow. The number of segments should be decided by how many different things your organisation can actually execute — which is usually three or four, occasionally five, and never nine. If marketing can run three campaigns a quarter, a nine-segment solution is a nine-segment solution in name only.

Naming and sizing

A cluster number is not a segment. “Cluster 3” tells a marketing team nothing they can use, and it will not survive its first contact with a steering committee. The work that converts one into the other is profiling: describing each group in the language of the business, sizing it, and attaching a decision and a person to it.

profile <- bank |>
  group_by(segment) |>
  summarise(
    customers      = n(),
    share          = n() / nrow(bank),
    med_tenure     = median(tenure_months),
    med_balance    = median(avg_balance_ngn),
    med_products   = median(products_held),
    med_engagement = median(digital_engagement_score),
    churn_rate     = mean(churned_90d),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  arrange(desc(med_balance))

Note that the summary uses median() rather than mean() for balance, tenure and products. In a distribution with a long tail, the mean is pulled upwards by a small number of very large customers and stops describing anybody in particular; the median tells you about the customer in the middle of the group, which is the customer the campaign will actually meet.

Then the table that goes in the deck. This is the deliverable; the chart beside it is supporting evidence.

Segment Size Median balance Tenure Complaints 90-day churn Churners The action Owner
Anchors — long tenure, most products, highest balances 2,977 · 25% ₦1,694k 95 mo 0.10 3.8% 113 RM review, quarterly Head of Affluent
Digital natives — highest engagement, mid balance 3,411 · 28% ₦541k 71 mo 0.18 5.9% 201 In-app cross-sell, no human contact Digital
At risk — complaints, mid engagement 2,100 · 18% ₦422k 58 mo 1.22 20.1% 423 Outbound call within 30 days Retention
Dormant mass — low on everything 3,512 · 29% ₦261k 44 mo 0.00 15.3% 536 Serve digitally, at minimum cost Operations

Read along one row and you will see what a segmentation deliverable contains: a name a human being would actually use, a size in both customers and share, the numbers that justify the action, the action itself, and the function that owns it. A table with those columns can be argued with, resourced and reviewed in six months. A table of cluster centroids cannot.

That table is generated by the same script that draws the figure above it, from the recipe printed above, and it is not typed by hand. There is a reason for that, and it is not tidiness — the failure it prevents is one that any careful team can walk into.

An earlier version of this page carried a hand-typed table under the same figure, and the two disagreed on every single cell. The chart said Anchors were 2,505 customers at 3.2% churn while the table said 2,977 at 3.8%. Both sets of numbers looked entirely plausible — same order of magnitude, same qualitative story, neither resembling a typing error. Nobody noticed for weeks, and there was no reason anybody should have: checking a figure against the sentence beside it is not a step that appears in anybody’s review process.

The instructive part is what happened next. The obvious repair was to regenerate the table from the figure, and that is what was done — which made the visible contradiction disappear and left the real one in place, because the figure was the artefact that had drifted. It had quietly been built on five features while this page’s recipe selects six, so the corrected table was now the honest output of a clustering that this page never asks for, and the redundancy box below became false as a result. It took a second reviewer, re-running the recipe from scratch rather than comparing the two artefacts to each other, to find it.

Two lessons come out of that, and the second is the expensive one. One source, and every number in the prose computed from it — which is the whole subject of 4.3. And: when two artefacts disagree, establish which one is wrong before you make them agree. Reconciling to whichever is more convenient to change is how an error gets promoted from visible to invisible, and an invisible error is a great deal more expensive than a contradiction somebody can see.

Read the table before you name anything

Three things in that table are worth more than the clustering that produced them, and all three are visible without any statistics at all.

Median balance runs from ₦261k to ₦1.69m — a factor of 6.5. In other words the typical customer in the top segment holds about six and a half times the balance of the typical customer in the bottom one. These segments genuinely differ on value, which means value-based names are earned rather than merely asserted. Check that before you name anything: a segment called “Premium” whose median balance turns out to be within a rounding error of “Mass” is how a segmentation loses a room, and it happens more often than the published case studies admit.

The highest-balance segment has the lowest churn — 3.8% against a 10.6% base. That is not really a surprise once you look at what sits underneath it. Tenure, products held and balance all move together, and each of them independently makes leaving less likely: a customer of eight years with four products and a salary account has a great deal of friction between them and the door. But the commercial consequence is worth stating plainly, because it runs against instinct: your most valuable segment is not your most at-risk one, and a retention budget aimed at “our best customers” would be aimed at the people least likely to leave.

Now read the churn column and the churner column against each other. They are not the same question and they do not give the same answer. “At risk” has the highest rate at 20.1% — one in five of them goes in ninety days. But “Dormant mass” produces the highest count, at 536 leavers against 423 — a lower rate applied to a larger segment. Rate answers where is the problem worst? Count answers where are the customers we are actually losing? A retention team with capacity for a fixed number of calls a month needs the second, weighted by what each departure is worth — which is precisely the ranking 3.5 builds this afternoon.

And the Dormant mass leaves quietly, because each departure takes ₦261k of balance with it rather than ₦1.69m — and there are a lot of them, at 29% of the base. Whether that matters at all is a commercial judgement about cost-to-serve rather than a statistical one: if those accounts cost more to maintain than they contribute, their departure is not a problem to be solved. That judgement is exactly what this table exists to provoke, and it is not one the clustering can make for you.

The fifth thing that goes wrong, and it is live in this dataset

digital_engagement_score and txns_last_90d correlate at 0.86. Read that as: customers who transact a lot score highly on digital engagement, almost without exception. The two columns have different names and different units, but they are two measurements of the same underlying thing — how active this customer is.

That is a problem specific to distance-based methods, and it takes a moment to see. Distance is computed by adding up the difference on every feature. Standardise all six columns and cluster on Euclidean distance, and engagement contributes twice — two of six features, a third of the total weight in the distance metric, for one concept. Complaints, tenure and products each get one share; activity gets two. Nothing in the output mentions this.

Look at what it does in practice. Measure how far apart the four clusters sit on each standardised feature — the range between the highest and lowest cluster centre — and you get:

Feature Between-cluster range
complaints_last_12m 2.25
txns_last_90d 1.75
digital_engagement_score 1.73
avg_balance_ngn (log) 1.51
products_held 1.20
tenure_months 1.03

The two bolded rows are the same concept, sitting side by side. Together they come to 3.48, which outweighs complaints at 2.25 — and complaints is the single strongest genuine separator in the list. One idea is doing two features’ work, and it is quietly the most influential thing in the model. The segments you are about to name are shaped by that, and to that extent they are partly an artefact of how the feature list was assembled rather than a fact about the customers.

The check is cheap and takes about a minute: compute the correlation matrix of your features before you cluster, and look at it. Where two columns correlate above roughly 0.8, drop one of them or combine them into a single index. This kind of redundancy quietly reweighting the distance metric is at least as common as failing to scale, and considerably harder to see, because the output of a redundant clustering looks exactly like the output of a good one.

Stability — the question nobody asks

Here is the question that decides whether any of this survives contact with next quarter. You are going to run a campaign against these segments in three months’ time. Will the segments still be there?

The concern is not philosophical. k-means will return k groups from any data you hand it, including data with no group structure at all — that is what the algorithm does. So a segmentation can be perfectly well fitted and still be a description of noise, and the only way to tell the two apart is to check whether the structure reappears when you look again.

The cheapest version of that check is to split your customer base in half at random, cluster each half independently, and ask whether the two halves found the same thing.

# Split the base in half at random, cluster each half separately,
# and see whether you get the same structure.
set.seed(7)
half <- sample(c(TRUE, FALSE), nrow(X), replace = TRUE)

fit_a <- kmeans(X[half, ],  centers = 4, nstart = 50)
fit_b <- kmeans(X[!half, ], centers = 4, nstart = 50)

# Cluster labels are ARBITRARY between fits. fit_a$centers and fit_b$centers
# are in unrelated row orders, so comparing them row by row is meaningless.
# Score both halves with ONE set of centres and compare the partitions.
assign_to <- function(centres, X) {
  apply(as.matrix(X), 1, \(row) which.min(colSums((t(centres) - row)^2)))
}

lab_a <- assign_to(fit_a$centers, X)          # everyone, by half A's centres
lab_b <- assign_to(fit_b$centers, X)          # everyone, by half B's centres

mclust::adjustedRandIndex(lab_a, lab_b)
# 1.0 = identical partitions. Above ~0.7 the structure is real.
# Below ~0.4 you have found noise, and the campaign will not replicate.

The comment in the middle of that block is the part people get wrong, so it is worth saying in prose as well. Cluster numbers mean nothing between two separate fits: what half A calls cluster 1 may be what half B calls cluster 3, because labels are assigned in whatever order the algorithm happened to converge. Comparing the two sets of centres row by row therefore compares two unrelated orderings. The way round it is to score everybody twice — once using half A’s centres, once using half B’s — and then ask how much the two groupings agree about which customers belong together. That agreement measure is the adjusted Rand index, and the adjustment in its name is for chance: two random groupings score around zero rather than around some misleadingly positive number.

If two random halves of your base produce materially different partitions, you have found structure in noise, and the campaign built on it will not replicate. The check takes about four minutes and almost nobody runs it — not through carelessness, but because by that point everybody has moved on to naming the groups, which is the more enjoyable part.

The second stability question is about time rather than sampling. Re-run the segmentation on data from six months ago and count how many customers land in a different segment. A high rate of movement is not a failure; it tells you your segmentation is measuring a state rather than a type, and the two are used differently. State-based segments need re-scoring monthly and belong in an operational system; type-based segments can be assigned once and left alone in the CRM.

Core lab · 30 minutes

  • Run RFM on the banking base. Collapse to four actionable groups. Write the action for each.
  • Now run k-means properly: variables chosen, balance logged, everything scaled.
  • Fit k from 2 to 9, look at the elbow, then choose k on execution grounds and defend it.
  • Profile, name and size your segments. Build the six-column table above.
  • Run the split-half stability check, then compare your k-means segments with your RFM groups. Did the model earn its keep?

That last comparison is the one to protect if you run short of time. It is entirely possible for the answer to be “no, the model did not earn its keep” — and finding that out in thirty minutes, with nothing at stake, is a considerably better outcome than finding it out after a quarter of campaign spend.

Stretch

Segment something other than customers. The technique is identical, the data is usually already available, and the applications are badly under-used:

  • SKUs by demand pattern — fast and steady, fast and spiky, slow and lumpy, dying. Each of those needs a different replenishment policy, and getting the classification right is usually worth more than any improvement to the forecast itself.
  • Suppliers by lead-time reliability and spend. Consolidate, dual-source, exit.
  • Depots by growth and margin. Invest, hold, fix, close.
  • Employees by flight risk and replacement cost — with a Friday conversation attached about whether you should, which 5.4 takes up properly.

Pick one from your own business and do it.

And, if you have ten minutes: refit the bank segmentation with txns_last_90d dropped, so that one concept carries one feature’s weight instead of two. Put the two profile tables side by side and compare them honestly.

Two things are worth noticing, and the first is not what the warning above would lead you to expect. The segment sizes move by a few hundred customers and the balance ordering survives intact — clustering is more robust to this kind of redundancy than the warning implies, which is precisely why the error is so easy to leave in place. If it produced obvious nonsense, somebody would have caught it.

What does rearrange is the tenure profile. The highest-balance group stops being the longest-tenured, and a mid-value segment overtakes it by about two and a half years. If you had named your segments on tenure, or written a paragraph in the deck about your best customers also being your oldest, you would now be rewriting those sentences.

That is the honest size of the effect: the redundancy critique rarely changes who is in which group, and it regularly changes the story you tell about them. Both halves of that sentence are worth carrying into your own work.

Copilot check

Ask an assistant to “segment my customers” and it will produce clean, well-structured k-means code, and it will usually get the mechanics right. The gaps are not in the code; they are in the judgements the code cannot make.

Three things to check every time:

  1. Did it scale? Sometimes yes, often not, and it will not warn you either way. This is the single highest-value line to look for in generated clustering code.
  2. Did it choose k with a silhouette score or an elbow, and present that as the answer? The statistically optimal k is not the commercially executable k. The assistant has no way of knowing how many distinct campaigns your marketing team can run in a quarter, and it will not think to ask.
  3. Did it name the segments? It will cheerfully invent evocative names from the cluster centres — “The Aspirational Urbanite” — with complete confidence and no knowledge of your market. Names carry meaning inside an organisation, and they outlive the analysis that produced them. Write them yourself.

The prompt that works better does two things: it specifies the preparation, and it explicitly withholds the judgements.

Cluster this data. Requirements: log-transform the skewed monetary variables, standardise everything, fit k from 2 to 9, and report both the within-cluster sum of squares and a profile table of the segment medians for each k. Do not choose k for me and do not name the segments — I will do both. Then run a split-half stability check.

Takeaways

  1. The test: would you do something different for each segment? Write the sentence first.
  2. Run RFM first. It is the benchmark the model must beat.
  3. Scale, and transform skewed variables, or you have clustered one column.
  4. Choose the number of segments by what your organisation can execute, not by the elbow.
  5. Check split-half stability before anyone builds a campaign on it.
