Forecasting with intervals

Trend, season, break, backtest — and what 80% actually promises — Session 3.3, 60 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can produce a forecast that comes with an honest range rather than a single number; test it against periods it has never seen; explain in one sentence what an “80% interval” actually promises, in language a CFO will accept without further translation; and recognise the moment when a business series changes level, so that you handle it deliberately instead of averaging across it. None of that requires you to write the code yourself; it requires you to know what to ask for, and what a good answer looks like when it comes back.

If your Tuesday pipeline did not finish

Everything below reads outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet — the deduplicated, null-resolved, dimension-joined table that Tuesday’s lab produces.

A reference build of that exact file ships with the course data, in outputs/. If your own pipeline is not finished, point today’s code at the reference build and carry on. You will fix your pipeline at the next break or this evening; you will not spend this morning catching up while the room moves on without you.

Nobody is marked down for this. The capstone checkpoint asks whether your pipeline runs by Friday, not whether it ran by Wednesday at nine.

A forecast without an interval is a wish

Almost every forecast that reaches a management meeting arrives as one number. Next quarter’s revenue will be ₦2.4bn. It is precise, it fits in a cell, and it is the shape the meeting expects. It is also, on its own, close to useless for planning — and the reason is worth working through rather than asserting, because it is the foundation of everything else here.

Consider a planner deciding how much stock to hold. Told only that the forecast is ₦2.4bn, they know what somebody expects and nothing at all about how wrong that expectation might be. Should they cover ₦2.5bn of demand? ₦3bn? The single number cannot say, so in practice they add a margin based on how burnt they were last time — a decision made with information nobody wrote down.

Now give them the same forecast expressed differently: ₦2.4bn, with an 80% interval running from ₦1.9bn to ₦2.9bn. That is ₦500m of downside and ₦500m of upside around the central figure, and it changes the conversation completely. The planner now knows the shortfall they must survive, the upside they should be equipped to serve, and the width of everybody’s ignorance. A range half that wide would justify a leaner inventory position; a range twice as wide would tell them the forecast is not, on its own, a sound basis for the decision at all.

Which is why the sentence worth carrying out of this session is this one:

The interval is the forecast. The central line is a convenience.

Organisations that forecast without intervals do not eliminate uncertainty. They relocate it. The uncertainty that went unstated in the forecast reappears three months later in the variance report, where it has become an argument about whose fault the gap was — and by then nobody can distinguish a plan that was wrong from a plan that was right about a range the outcome happened to land at the edge of.

What an 80% interval promises

An interval attached to a forecast is called a prediction interval, and the promise it makes is narrower than most people assume. Here is the whole of it, in one sentence:

“If we produced a hundred forecasts like this one, we would expect the actual outcome to fall inside the stated range about eighty times.”

Eighty times inside, twenty times outside — one in five. That is the claim, and it is a claim about the procedure rather than about this particular quarter. Note carefully what it does not say.

  • It does not say the central line is likely. It is not, and this surprises people. On a continuous quantity, almost every specific value is unlikely; the central line is simply the value with the most probability packed around it.
  • It does not say the true value is equally likely anywhere in the range. Outcomes near the middle are more likely than outcomes near the edges, which is why “we came in at the bottom of the range” is a different kind of result from “we came in at the middle”.
  • It does not cover events the model has never seen. A currency devaluation, a regulatory change, a port closure, a competitor exiting the market. The interval is calculated from how much the series has varied in the past, so intervals are conditional on the future resembling the past — and saying that out loud is precisely what separates a forecast from a promise.

That last point is the one to say in the meeting, in those words. It is also why the 95% interval is usually the wrong one to show: widening from 80% to 95% buys a range so broad that it stops constraining any decision, and it still does not cover the things that actually go wrong, because those are the ones with no precedent in the data. An 80% interval is narrow enough to plan against and honest about being beaten one time in five.

Four components, in order

Before fitting anything, take the series apart and look at the pieces. Any business series can be thought of as the sum of four things, and naming them gives you somewhere to direct your attention.

Level — where the series sits: the typical weekly volume, ignoring everything else. Trend — where it is going, over months and years. Season — the pattern that repeats on a fixed cycle: higher in December, lower in August, a weekly rhythm, a monthly billing cycle. Remainder — everything left once level, trend and season have been accounted for.

Separating a series into those parts is called decomposition, and STL below is one standard way of doing it. It produces one panel per component, stacked over the same time axis.

library(tidyverse)
library(fable)
library(tsibble)
library(feasts)

weekly <- arrow::read_parquet("outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet") |>
  group_by(week_starting) |>
  summarise(units = sum(units_sold, na.rm = TRUE)) |>
  as_tsibble(index = week_starting)

# Look at the pieces before you fit anything
weekly |>
  model(STL(units ~ season(window = "periodic"))) |>
  components() |>
  autoplot()

The remainder panel is the one to read carefully, and it is the one most people skip past on their way to the trend. The remainder is supposed to be featureless — the leftovers after the structure has been removed. So if you can see something in it, the decomposition has failed to account for something real, and that something is usually interesting.

Three patterns are worth recognising by sight. A level shift — the remainder sitting mostly above zero for a stretch and then mostly below — means the series changed level and the model absorbed the change as noise. A run of same-signed errors means the model is systematically under- or over-predicting through that period. And growing variance, where the scatter widens over time, means the series has become more volatile, so an interval estimated on the whole history will be too narrow at the end.

Random scatter with no visible structure is the good outcome: the decomposition has captured what there was to capture, and what remains is genuinely unpredictable.

Fit several. Never one.

The most common forecasting error in business is not choosing the wrong model. It is fitting one model and believing it, with nothing to compare it against.

The remedy is to fit several at once, including some that are deliberately unsophisticated, and let them compete on evidence. Modern forecasting packages make this almost free — the block below fits five in a single statement.

fit <- weekly |>
  model(
    naive    = NAIVE(units),                          # last value. The benchmark.
    seasonal = SNAIVE(units ~ lag("year")),           # same week last year
    ets      = ETS(units),                            # exponential smoothing
    arima    = ARIMA(units),
    ensemble = combination_model(ETS(units), ARIMA(units))
  )

fc <- fit |> forecast(h = "13 weeks")

fc |>
  autoplot(weekly |> filter(week_starting >= as.Date("2025-01-01")), level = c(80)) +
  labs(title = "Thirteen-week unit forecast, five models, 80% intervals",
       subtitle = "If the naive benchmark is competitive, the sophisticated model is not earning its keep")

Five names, in plain language, because knowing what each one assumes is more useful than knowing how it is implemented:

  • NAIVE predicts that next week will equal this week. No parameters, no judgement; it carries the last observation forward.
  • SNAIVE — seasonal naive — predicts that this week will equal the same week last year. On a series with a strong annual pattern this is a surprisingly strong rule.
  • ETS stands for error, trend, season: exponential smoothing, which estimates level, trend and seasonal pattern as weighted averages where recent observations count for more.
  • ARIMA is the other classical family, modelling the series in terms of its own recent values and its own recent errors.
  • combination_model averages two forecasts. Averaging several reasonable models is one of the most reliable free improvements available, because their errors are partly independent and averaging cancels some of them.

Now look at what happens when this is done honestly against data the models were not shown.

Sit with that figure for a moment longer than is comfortable, because it is not the figure a forecasting session usually shows. Sessions like this one normally end with a chart in which the clever model tracks the outcome closely. This one does the opposite, and it is more useful for it.

Start with what went right. The interval behaves exactly as an interval should. The band widens as the horizon lengthens, which is correct — the further ahead you look, the less you know — and it covers 85% of the held-out weeks. Eighty-five per cent of thirteen weeks is eleven of them: eleven weeks inside the 80% band and two outside, against an expectation of about ten or eleven inside. The interval was honest.

Now what went wrong. The central line is wrong all quarter, running 7.2% high in every single week — not high on average with weeks either side, but high every week. That persistence is the diagnostic. Errors that scatter around zero are noise; errors that all point the same way are a missing ingredient.

And the “improvement” made it worse. The refinement was to add a drift term: instead of predicting that this week equals the same week last year, predict that it equals the same week last year adjusted upwards for the growth seen recently. That is a reasonable thing to try, and it is what most analysts would try next. It made the forecast 4.6 times worse than the plain rule it was built on — its average error was four and a half times the simpler rule’s.

The reason is instructive. The drift term compares the last 26 weeks against the same 26 weeks a year earlier, and that comparison runs straight across the November 2025 northern break. The recent period contains the reduced volumes; the year-earlier period does not. The rule cannot distinguish a business that is growing from a business that lost a chunk of demand at a point in time, so it reads a level shift as a growth rate and projects it forward for thirteen weeks.

Three things follow, and between them they are the session.

  1. The benchmark beat the model. Publish that. It is the most useful result available in forecasting and the one people are most reluctant to report, because it feels like arriving at a review having done nothing. It is the opposite: it is the finding that stops an organisation buying complexity that does not pay.
  2. A wide interval rescued a wrong central line. Published as a point estimate, this would have had the business plan a quarter around a number it never once achieved. Published with its interval, nobody was misled about how much was known.
  3. The break is upstream of all of it. No forecasting tool finds it. You find it in 3.1, by plotting the series and asking what is odd here.

Always fit the naive benchmark

NAIVE predicts that next week equals this week. SNAIVE predicts that this week equals the same week last year. Neither of them took any skill, any data science, or any budget.

That is the entire point. They give you a floor. If a sophisticated model cannot beat both of them on data it has never seen, whatever it is doing is not worth the licence fee, the maintenance or the meeting — and you have found that out in ten seconds rather than after two quarters of quiet under-performance.

So report the benchmark alongside your model, every time, on its own row of the table. It is the single most credibility-building line in a forecasting presentation, because it shows the audience that you went looking for evidence against your own work before they had to.

For anybody commissioning a forecast rather than building one, this becomes one pleasant question: “How does this compare with just assuming next quarter looks like the same quarter last year?” If the answer is a number, you are in good hands. If nobody has checked, the check takes an afternoon and is worth having before the forecast is used.

Backtesting: the only evidence that counts

How well a model fits the data it was built on tells you almost nothing about how well it will forecast, and it is worth being concrete about why. A model with enough flexibility can be made to pass very close to every point in the history it was shown. That is not skill; it is memory.

The measure of a forecast is its performance on periods it has never seen, and the only way to obtain that measure honestly is to hide some of your own data from the model, forecast the hidden period, and compare. Doing that once is called a holdout. Doing it repeatedly, at many different cut-off points in the history, is called backtesting, or rolling-origin cross-validation, and it is the version that gives you an answer you can rely on.

# Rolling origin, properly: refit at many cut-offs, forecast 13 weeks from each,
# and average the error across all of them. One holdout is one draw; this is
# the distribution.
folds <- weekly |>
  tsibble::stretch_tsibble(.init = 78, .step = 13)   # grow the training window

accuracy_table <- folds |>
  model(naive  = NAIVE(units),
        snaive = SNAIVE(units ~ lag("year")),
        ets    = ETS(units),
        arima  = ARIMA(units)) |>
  forecast(h = 13) |>
  accuracy(weekly)

accuracy_table |>
  select(.model, MAE, RMSE, MAPE, MASE) |>
  arrange(MASE)

stretch_tsibble(.init = 78, .step = 13) does the work, and it is easier to picture than to read. It takes the first 78 weeks as a training set, fits every model to just that, and forecasts the next 13 weeks. Then it extends the training window by 13 weeks and does it again, and again, until it runs out of history. Each pass produces a forecast made with no knowledge of the period it is scored against — exactly the position you are in when you forecast next quarter.

Why the repetition? Because a single holdout is a sample of size one: it tells you how one model did on one quarter, and quarters differ — one with a promotion in it, one containing a break, one unusually quiet. Roll the origin forward and you get the same comparison at every cut-off in your history. That is the difference between “our model beat naive” and “our model beats naive”: one is an anecdote, the other is a property.

Which error measure to read

The table above reports four measures, and choosing between them matters more than it looks.

Read MASE, not MAPE. MAPE — mean absolute percentage error — is the measure everybody quotes, and it is badly behaved in exactly the situations commercial data produces. It takes each week’s error as a percentage of that week’s actual value, which creates three problems. It is undefined when the actual is zero. It explodes when the actual is merely small: miss by 5 units on a week that sold 10 and you have a 50% error, but miss by the same 5 units on a week that sold 2 and MAPE records 250%. And it penalises over- and under-forecasting asymmetrically, because a forecast can be more than 100% above the actual but never more than 100% below it. For a business with stockout weeks and slow-moving SKUs, all three are live rather than theoretical.

MASE — mean absolute scaled error — avoids all of that by dividing your average error by the error a simple naive rule would have made on the training data. That denominator is what makes the measure comparable across series of wildly different sizes: a MASE of 0.8 means the same thing on a series measured in units and a series measured in billions of naira.

The half people get wrong when they explain it is which errors are being compared. Read it as: below 1, your out-of-sample error is smaller than the naive rule’s in-sample error — a demanding bar. Above 1, it is not. That is not quite the same statement as “we beat naive on the holdout”, because the two sides of the ratio come from different periods, and over a long horizon the two comparisons can genuinely disagree. Which is why the accuracy table reports the benchmarks on their own rows: you can read both comparisons directly rather than infer one from the other.

The break

Everything so far has treated the network total. Now fit the same models to the northern region alone — the series that stepped down in November — and watch what a well-behaved model does when the ground moves underneath it.

north <- arrow::read_parquet("outputs/fmcg_weekly.parquet") |>
  filter(region %in% c("North West", "North Central")) |>
  group_by(week_starting) |>
  summarise(units = sum(units_sold, na.rm = TRUE)) |>
  as_tsibble(index = week_starting)

north |>
  model(ets = ETS(units)) |>
  forecast(h = "13 weeks") |>
  autoplot(north, level = 80)

Before opening the panel, predict what the forecast will look like. Where will the central line sit relative to the recent weeks, and will the interval be wider or narrower than you would want?

What goes wrong, and the three ways to handle it

The northern series drops 29% in a single week — the week of 3 November 2025 — and stays down. Over the whole post-period it sits about 28% below where it was, against a generator that removed exactly 37% of northern demand.

Those three numbers are all different, and knowing which one to quote is itself the skill. The week-on-week step measures the discontinuity at the moment it happened; the level shift compares the average of everything before with the average of everything after; the underlying effect is what was actually done to the business. They answer three different questions — how big was the jolt?, how different is the period since?, and how much demand was removed? — and a forecaster who uses them interchangeably will be caught out by whichever one the audience meant.

A model fitted across the whole history does not see two regimes. It sees one series with a great deal of variance in it, and behaves accordingly. Three consequences follow, all visible in the chart:

  • it forecasts a level between the old and new regimes — too high, because the older, higher observations still carry weight;
  • it produces an interval far too wide, because it is trying to account for a one-off shift as though it were ordinary week-to-week noise, which inflates its estimate of how much this series normally moves;
  • and it will be wrong in the same direction for the entire horizon, which is the signature of a missed structural break and is quite different from being wrong by a similar amount in random directions.

There are three legitimate responses, in ascending order of effort.

  1. Refit on the post-break period only. Fewer observations and a wider interval as a result, but an honest level. Usually right when the break is a permanent structural change — a depot closed, a customer left, a territory was reassigned. Say in the note how many observations you kept: a forecast built on fifteen weeks deserves to be read differently from one built on three years.
  2. Include the break as a term. ARIMA(units ~ step_dummy) adds an intervention variable: a column that is 0 before the break and 1 afterwards, which the model can use to represent the shift explicitly instead of absorbing it as variance. You keep the whole history, and the model is told that something specific happened rather than left to infer it.
  3. Forecast the two regimes separately where the break has split the business into genuinely different populations, which is what happened here.
# Option 2, in code: an intervention variable the model can use, rather than
# variance it has to absorb.
north <- north |> mutate(resegmented = week_starting >= as.Date("2025-11-03"))

north |>
  model(with_break = ARIMA(units ~ resegmented)) |>
  forecast(new_data = new_data(north, 13) |> mutate(resegmented = TRUE))

Read that block as two steps. The first creates a TRUE/FALSE column marking the weeks after the break. The second tells the model to use it — and then, when forecasting, states that the future weeks are also on the far side of the break, which is the line people forget.

What is never acceptable is fitting across the break and not mentioning it. The number will be wrong, the interval will be wrong, and the error will be one-directional and persistent — the kind that damages a forecaster’s credibility most, because by the time it is obvious it has been wrong in the same direction every week for a quarter. Disclosed in advance it is a modelling choice with a caveat attached; discovered afterwards it looks like something hidden.

How you find it: you plot the series. Session 3.1. There is no substitute, and there is no tool that removes the need.

Judgemental adjustment, and when it is legitimate

Planners override forecasts. Analysts often treat this as interference, and they are often wrong to, because the planner frequently knows things the history cannot contain: the promotion agreed for week 6, the new listing that starts in week 9, the customer who gave notice last Thursday. Refusing overrides on principle throws away real information. The question is not whether to allow them but how to keep them useful, and two rules do most of the work.

Log every override: who made it, when, how much they moved the number, and why. Then, a quarter later, measure them — what the forecast said, what the override said, what actually happened. In organisations that do this, roughly half of overrides improve accuracy and half degrade it, and the split is strikingly consistent by individual: some people reliably add information and others reliably add noise. That is enormously useful management information, it costs almost nothing to collect, and hardly anybody collects it.

Override the level, not the interval. Adjusting the central case on the basis of something you know is legitimate judgement. Narrowing the range because you feel confident is almost always overconfidence, and it removes exactly the protection that made the forecast safe to plan against. If new information genuinely reduces uncertainty — the promotion is now signed rather than proposed — say so explicitly, and let somebody else agree the range should narrow.

Core lab · 25 minutes

  • Build the total-network weekly series. Decompose it. Read the remainder panel.
  • Fit five models including both naive benchmarks. Backtest on a 13-week holdout, rank by MASE, and say how many models beat naive.
  • Now the northern series. Fit it ignoring the break, then handle the break one of the three ways. Report both intervals and how much narrower the honest one is.
  • Prepare your forecast market entry: one depot, quarter ahead, 80% interval.

The second item has a specific answer and it is worth writing it down rather than glancing at it, because “how many models beat naive?” is the question you will be asking of other people’s forecasts for the rest of your career, and the number is often smaller than expected.

Stretch

Forecast at the SKU–depot level rather than the total, then add the individual forecasts together. Compare that sum with a direct forecast of the total.

They will not match, and the reason repays understanding: aggregating forecasts is not the same as forecasting an aggregate. Individual series are noisier than their total, and their errors are correlated rather than independent, so the two routes to a national number arrive at different places. This is the hierarchical forecasting reconciliation problem, and it is precisely the problem your S&OP process has every month when the sum of the regional plans does not equal the national plan — usually resolved by somebody adjusting a spreadsheet until the numbers tie.

There is a formal solution rather than a manual one (fable’s reconcile() with min_trace), which finds the adjustments that keep the hierarchy consistent while disturbing the individual forecasts as little as possible. For anyone who runs a planning cycle, it is a genuine competitive advantage and almost nobody uses it.

Copilot check

Ask an assistant to “forecast this time series” and you will get a fitted model, a chart and a number, quickly and competently, and the code will usually be correct. What is missing is not technique but scepticism, in four consistent places.

  1. A naive benchmark. Without one you have no idea whether the model added anything at all over assuming next week looks like last week.
  2. A backtest. It will typically report how well the model fits the data it was trained on, which as we have seen is close to meaningless as evidence about the future.
  3. The break. It will not notice one unless it can run code and plot the series — in which case it often will. Ask for it explicitly rather than hoping.
  4. The caveat. It will rarely volunteer that the interval is conditional on the future resembling the past, which is the sentence that keeps the forecast honest in the meeting.

None of that is a defect in the assistant so much as a consequence of the request. “Forecast this series” asks for a forecast, and it obligingly produces one. Ask for the evidence as well and it will produce that too:

Forecast this weekly series 13 weeks ahead. Requirements: fit NAIVE and SNAIVE benchmarks alongside ETS and ARIMA; evaluate with rolling-origin cross-validation on a 13-week horizon and report MASE; check for structural breaks and tell me if you find one; produce 80% intervals; and state in one sentence what the interval assumes.

That prompt is worth keeping. Every requirement in it is something this session argued for, which means you have both the checklist and the reasons behind it — and the reasons are what let you adapt it when the next request is not a weekly series thirteen weeks ahead.

Takeaways

  1. The interval is the forecast. The central line is a convenience.
  2. Always fit the naive benchmarks. Report MASE, not MAPE.
  3. Backtest on periods the model has never seen, repeatedly.
  4. Handle breaks explicitly. Fitting across one produces persistent, one-directional error.
  5. Intervals are conditional on the future resembling the past. Say so out loud.
