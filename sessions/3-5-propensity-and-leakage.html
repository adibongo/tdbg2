Propensity and the leak

The churn model that scored 0.98, and why that should have frightened you — Session 3.5, 45 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can build a propensity model, evaluate it in business terms rather than statistical ones, choose an action threshold from capacity and economics, and — above all — detect target leakage, which is the single most common reason a model that performed brilliantly in development performs terribly in production.

Build it first, and let it be too good

A propensity model is simply a model that estimates, for each customer, the probability that something will happen — that they will leave, buy, default or respond. It produces a number between 0 and 1 for every row, and the whole commercial value of it lies in what you do with that ranking afterwards.

Build one now, before reading further. It matters that you see the result before you see the explanation.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidymodels)

bank <- read_csv("data/bank_customers.csv") |>
  mutate(churned = factor(churned_90d, levels = c(1, 0), labels = c("yes", "no")))

set.seed(2026)
split <- initial_split(bank, prop = 0.75, strata = churned)
train <- training(split); test <- testing(split)

rec <- recipe(churned ~ tenure_months + age + products_held + avg_balance_ngn +
                digital_engagement_score + txns_last_90d + complaints_last_12m +
                salary_domiciled + nps_last_survey + segment +
                closure_form_started,                 # <- everything available
              data = train) |>
  step_log(avg_balance_ngn, base = 10) |>
  step_dummy(all_nominal_predictors()) |>
  step_normalize(all_numeric_predictors())

fit <- workflow() |>
  add_recipe(rec) |>
  add_model(logistic_reg() |> set_engine("glm")) |>
  fit(train)

augment(fit, test) |> roc_auc(churned, .pred_yes)
#> 0.984

The number at the bottom is the AUC, or area under the ROC curve. In plain terms it answers one question: if you pick a customer who churned and a customer who did not, entirely at random, how often does the model give the churner the higher score? An AUC of 0.5 means the model is doing no better than a coin, and 1.0 means it never gets that comparison wrong.

0.984, then, means the model wins that comparison roughly ninety-eight times in a hundred. In a churn context that is extraordinary. Most production churn models in retail banking live between 0.70 and 0.80, and a team that moved from 0.74 to 0.79 would reasonably regard it as a good quarter’s work.

Which is the moment to slow down rather than to write the email. A result that far outside the normal range is telling you something, and the something is usually about the data rather than the modelling.

Before anything else, run the leakage test

The only leakage test you need

Take the list of features, go down it one at a time, and ask a single question of each:

Would I have known this at the moment I needed to act?

Note what the question is not. It is not “is this correlated with the outcome” — you hope everything is. It is not “is this statistically significant”. It is a question about time: at the moment you would score the customer and decide whether to call them, does this value exist yet, in the system, in the form the model saw it?

Going down the column list one at a time takes about two minutes, and it is the highest return on two minutes anywhere in this course.

Feature Known at scoring time?
tenure_months Yes
age, segment, branch_state Yes
products_held, avg_balance_ngn Yes
digital_engagement_score, txns_last_90d Yes
complaints_last_12m Yes
salary_domiciled Yes
nps_last_survey Yes, if the survey predates the scoring date — check
closure_form_started No. The form is filled the week they leave.

The last row is the one that matters. A closure form is not a predictor of leaving; it is part of the act of leaving. Its presence in the training table is not evidence of carelessness by whoever built the extract — they were asked for the customer record and they supplied it, complete. The column belongs in the warehouse. It simply does not belong in a model that has to run before the customer has decided anything.

This is what target leakage means: information about the outcome has leaked backwards into the features, so the model is partly being told the answer rather than predicting it.

Drop the column and refit.

rec_clean <- rec |> step_rm(closure_form_started)
# ... refit ...
#> roc_auc 0.766

The AUC falls from 0.984 to 0.766 — from a result nobody in retail banking achieves to a result that is entirely ordinary. And the ordinary one is the real one. A model at 0.766 is useful, deployable, and roughly what the published literature on retail churn would lead you to expect. Nothing has been lost by removing the column, because the performance it was providing was never available at the moment you needed it.

Now do the thing that almost nobody does, and that turns this from an anecdote into a discipline. Simulate production. At the moment of scoring, nobody has started a closure form yet — so set that column to zero for everybody, re-score the held-out customers, and see what the leaked model would actually have done in service.

test_prod <- test |> mutate(closure_form_started = 0)
augment(fit, test_prod) |> roc_auc(churned, .pred_yes)
#> 0.768
augment(fit, test_prod) |> summarise(mean_predicted = mean(.pred_yes))
#> 0.009      <- against a true base rate of 0.106

What actually breaks is not what you expect

Look carefully at the first of those two numbers, because it is the counter-intuitive one. The leaked model’s ranking survives: AUC 0.768 in simulated production, essentially the same as the clean model’s 0.766. The order it puts customers in is still broadly right. If all you ever do with the scores is sort them and call the top 600, you might run this model for a year and never notice anything was wrong.

What collapses is calibration — the correspondence between a predicted probability and the real-world frequency it claims to describe. The model learned, quite correctly given its training data, that almost nobody churns unless a closure form has been started. Strip the form away and every predicted probability falls to the floor. Mean predicted risk comes out at 0.9% against a true base rate of 10.6%, so the model is understating the risk in the book by better than a factor of ten.

That is invisible until somebody multiplies the probability by something else — and sooner or later somebody always does. Expected value at risk, provisioning, a retention budget, an economic threshold: every one of those is a probability multiplied by a naira figure, and every one of them is now out by an order of magnitude while the model still looks fine on the metric everybody checks.

The general point is worth carrying away from the specific example. Discrimination and calibration are different properties of a model, and leakage can destroy one while leaving the other intact. If your model feeds a ranking, discrimination is what you need. If it feeds an arithmetic decision, calibration is what you need — and it is the one nobody reports.

Leakage does not announce itself as an error

It announces itself as success, and that is precisely what makes it so durable.

An error that produces a bad result gets investigated. Nobody investigates a result that exceeded expectations — there is no meeting scheduled for “the model worked better than we hoped”, and the natural response to 0.98 is congratulation rather than suspicion. That is not a failing of individual reviewers; it is how review processes are built, and it is why leakage survives them so reliably.

The habit worth installing, in yourself and in your team, is a small reversal of that instinct: an unexpectedly good result is a bug report until proven otherwise. It costs an hour when you are right and saves a deployment when you are not.

Four forms of leakage turn up often enough in real organisations to be worth recognising by shape:

  1. Post-outcome fields. Closure forms, exit interviews, cancellation reason codes, write-off flags. These are populated because the outcome happened, which is exactly the direction of causation you are trying to model.
  2. Updated-in-place fields. A customer_status column that gets overwritten each time it changes. Your historical training data then contains today’s status rather than the status as at the training date, and today’s status knows how the story ended.
  3. Aggregates computed over the whole period, including the future. An “average spend” figure that silently includes the months after the customer churned — during which they spent nothing — is a very effective churn predictor and a completely useless one.
  4. The identifier that encodes the answer. Account numbers issued in blocks, where a prefix happens to correspond to a closure batch. This sounds far-fetched until it happens to you.

Evaluate in business terms

AUC is a technical summary, and it belongs in the appendix. It is not a business case, and it is not the number you present, because nobody in the room can convert it into a decision.

Lift is the number that can be converted. If your retention team has capacity to call 600 customers a month, the only question that matters is: how many more churners do I reach in the top 600 by score than I would by calling 600 people at random?

scored <- augment(fit_clean, test) |>
  arrange(desc(.pred_yes)) |>
  mutate(rank = row_number(),
         decile = ntile(rank, 10),
         churner = churned == "yes")

base_rate <- mean(scored$churner)

scored |>
  group_by(decile) |>
  summarise(customers = n(),
            churners  = sum(churner),
            rate      = mean(churner),
            lift      = mean(churner) / base_rate)

That block sorts everybody by score, cuts the sorted list into ten equal groups, and reports the churn rate in each. Dividing each group’s rate by the overall base rate gives the lift: a lift of 1.0 means the group is no better than random, and a lift of 3.0 means you are finding three times as many churners there as chance would give you.

The right-hand panel is the cumulative gains curve, which answers the same question cumulatively: work down the ranked list and, at each point, what share of all the churners have you covered? Reaching roughly 66% of the churners in the top three deciles means that contacting 30% of the book finds two thirds of the people who were going to leave.

The top decile has a churn rate around 3.0 times the base rate. Translated out of the jargon: calling the top 10% by score finds three times as many at-risk customers as calling 10% at random. That sentence is what goes in the memo, because a reader can act on it without knowing what a model is. The AUC stays in the appendix, where somebody technical can check it.

Choosing the threshold

At some point the ranking has to become a rule: call this customer, do not call that one. The threshold that gets used by default is 0.5 — call anybody the model thinks is more likely than not to leave — and it is worth being clear that this number has no business content whatsoever. It is a mathematical midpoint, inherited from the way classification is taught, and it survives because nothing in the tooling prompts anybody to change it.

A defensible threshold comes from three things instead:

  1. Capacity. Six hundred calls a month is six hundred calls a month. If capacity is the binding constraint, the threshold is simply “the top 600 by score” and the probability attached to the 600th customer is incidental.
  2. Economics. What a call costs, fully loaded, against what a successful save is worth.
  3. Asymmetry. Missing a valuable churner costs far more than making an unnecessary call, and the ratio between those two is usually large.
# Threshold from economics rather than from convention.
cost_of_call        <- 2500          # ₦, fully loaded
value_of_save       <- 180000        # ₦, annual contribution
prob_call_works     <- 0.30          # measure this. Do not assume it.

# Call is worth it when:  p(churn) * value * effectiveness > cost
threshold <- cost_of_call / (value_of_save * prob_call_works)
round(threshold, 4)
#> 0.0463   -> call anyone above a 4.6% churn probability

The arithmetic is worth doing by hand once, because it is the kind of calculation a board can follow. A call costs ₦2,500. A save is worth ₦180,000 of annual contribution, but only 30% of calls actually save anybody, so the expected return on a call to a customer who is certain to leave is 180,000 × 0.30 = ₦54,000. Dividing the ₦2,500 cost by that ₦54,000 expected benefit gives 0.0463 — meaning the call breaks even at a churn probability of about 4.6%, and is profitable above it. Not 50%, and not by a small margin: an order of magnitude lower.

And now notice that the threshold does not bind

Apply that 4.6% cut-off to the book and something awkward emerges. 64% of the book scores above 4.6% — roughly 7,700 customers, against a stated capacity of 600 calls a month. The economic threshold selects nearly thirteen times more people than anyone can ring.

So the economic threshold is not really the cut-off. Capacity is. What the 4.6% figure actually tells you is how much capacity is costing you: every customer sitting between the 600th-ranked name and the 4.6% line is somebody it would have been profitable to call and you could not. Multiply that gap by the expected value per call and you have a costed business case for a larger retention team — which is a considerably more interesting number than the threshold, and one almost nobody computes because the threshold question feels like where the analysis ends.

Two rules follow, and they are worth settling before the argument starts:

  • When capacity binds, rank and take the top N. The probability is a sorting key and nothing more.
  • When capacity does not bind, the economic threshold is the cut-off — and you should be asking why you have idle retention capacity in the first place.

Work out which case you are in before you argue about where the line goes.

Three numbers, three different things

Deciding who to call actually requires three quantities, and it is easy to build only the first:

  • Probability of the event — will they leave?
  • Value at stake — does it matter to us if they do?
  • Probability the intervention works — can we do anything about it?

Most propensity models compute the first and are then deployed as though they had computed all three. The consequence is specific and predictable: a customer who is certain to leave and cannot be persuaded scores at the very top of a probability-ranked list, and is precisely the person least worth spending a call on. A naive high-probability-first targeting rule fills the call list with exactly those people, which is one reason retention programmes so often report disappointing effectiveness against a model that was working perfectly well.

Rank by the product of the three, not by the probability alone. The third term is the hardest to obtain, because it has to be measured with a holdout rather than assumed — which is Friday’s subject, in 5.2.

The survivorship trap next door

Leakage is about time. Its close relative is about membership: which rows made it into the file at all. Open b2b_funnel.csv and try to find what drives a win.

funnel <- read_csv("data/b2b_funnel.csv")

funnel |>
  filter(!is.na(sales_touchpoints)) |>
  summarise(cor = cor(sales_touchpoints, closed_won))

More touchpoints, more wins. The correlation is clear, and the conclusion looks obvious: tell the sales team to do more touchpoints. Before you do, work out what had to be true for a row to appear in that calculation at all.

Why that conclusion is worthless

sales_touchpoints is blank on 86% of rows. It was back-filled from the CRM only for opportunities that reached SQL stage.

The filter() in that first block therefore did more than drop some missing values. It restricted the analysis to a population defined by having already qualified. Of course those opportunities convert more often — qualification is what the filter selected on.

The question to ask of every dataset is this: what had to be true for a row to exist in this file? If the answer involves having already succeeded partway through the process, then every conclusion you draw is a conclusion about survivors.

funnel |>
  group_by(has_touchpoints = !is.na(sales_touchpoints)) |>
  summarise(n = n(), win_rate = mean(closed_won))

The win rate among rows with touchpoints recorded is 31%. Among rows without them it is 0% — not low, but exactly zero — because a lead that never reached SQL stage could not be won at all.

So the correlation is real, and it is real within a population defined by having already qualified. Both of those statements are true simultaneously, and that is what makes survivorship harder to spot than an ordinary error: nothing in the arithmetic is wrong, no assumption has been violated, and no diagnostic will flag it.

The question that finds it is not a statistical one. It is: what had to be true for a row to have this column filled in?

Core lab · 20 minutes

  • Build the churn model with everything. Record the AUC.
  • Run the leakage test on every column, refit clean, and note the size of the drop.
  • Build the decile lift table, and compute your threshold from capacity and economics rather than from 0.5.
  • Rank by probability × value × effectiveness and compare the top 600 to the probability-only top 600. How many names differ?

That final comparison is the one worth reporting back to the room. The two lists usually overlap much less than people expect, and the difference between them is the difference between a model that ranks risk and a model that ranks opportunity.

Stretch

Run the same exercise on hr_attrition.csv and find both traps:

  • the post-outcome column (exit_interview_booked, correlation 0.88 with the outcome), and
  • the strong, legitimate-looking predictor that is a proxy for a protected characteristic.

The second is the harder and more consequential of the two, because it does not fail any technical test at all — the feature is genuinely predictive, genuinely available at scoring time, and genuinely improves the model.

Then write the two-sentence answer you would give a risk officer who asks whether you would deploy it. Bring that answer to 5.4. We will read some of them out.

Copilot check

An assistant will build this model competently and will not detect the leak. That is not a weakness in the model; it is a boundary. Detecting the leak requires knowing when a business process fills a field, which is knowledge about how your operations run rather than knowledge about the shape of your data — and nothing in the file records it.

What it will do, if you supply that missing piece:

Here are the columns in my training data and a one-line description of what each one means and when it is populated in the business process. Identify any feature that could only be known after the outcome occurred, or that is a proxy for a protected characteristic. Explain your reasoning for each flag.

The load-bearing phrase there is “and when it is populated in the business process”. Supply it and the assistant becomes genuinely good at this, often catching columns a person would skim past. Withhold it and no amount of prompting will help, because the information simply is not present.

That generalises well beyond leakage: an assistant’s usefulness on any given task is roughly proportional to how much of the surrounding business context you were willing to type.

Takeaways

  1. An unexpectedly good result is a bug report until proven otherwise.
  2. The leakage test is one question about time: would I have known this in time?
  3. Report lift and gains, not AUC. Lift is a sentence a board understands.
  4. The threshold comes from capacity and economics. It is never 0.5.
  5. Rank by probability × value × effectiveness.
  6. Ask what had to be true for a row to exist in the file.
