Governance you can actually run

NDPA, GAID, model cards, and the policy you draft this afternoon — Session 5.4, 45 minutes

Aim

By the end of this session you can name the obligations that apply to analytics work in Nigeria, decide whether a given piece of work may leave your network, write a model card, and leave with a draft AI-use policy for your own function.

A necessary caveat

This session is a management briefing, not legal advice, and nobody delivering it is your lawyer. The obligations below are stated as at the time of writing and are cited so you can check them. Take the framework, take the questions, and take them to your legal and data protection functions.

Why this is on Friday and not in a compliance module

Governance has a reputation as the thing that happens to a project after the interesting work is finished, and that reputation is the reason it so often arrives too late to be useful. The practical case for putting it here, at the end of a week about building things, is simply this: governance is where analytics programmes get stopped, and being stopped in month seven is far more expensive than being slowed down in week one. In month seven you have a model, a team, a sponsor and a set of expectations, and unwinding any of them is costly. In week one you have a design document and a conversation.

There is a version of this session that reads out a statute, and it fails, because a list of sections is not something anybody can act on. The version that works organises the same material around three questions an executive genuinely faces, usually with somebody waiting for an answer:

  1. May this data leave my network?
  2. May this decision be made by a model?
  3. Who is accountable when it is wrong?

Everything below is in service of being able to answer those three quickly and defensibly.

The Nigerian frame, in the parts that bite

Two documents matter. The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 is the primary law — the thing that creates the obligations. The NDPC’s General Application and Implementation Directive (GAID) 2025 — issued 20 March 2025, effective 19 September 2025 after a six-month transition — is the operational detail: what you file, when, and at what tier. If you read only one, read the Act; if you have to act, you will need both.

Here is what an analytics function actually runs into.

Lawful basis, chosen and documented before you process. The starting point of the whole regime is that processing personal data requires a reason recognised in law, and that you should know which one you are relying on before you begin rather than after somebody asks. There are six: consent, contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest, vital interest, and public interest. Two things are worth internalising. “We already had the data” is not one of them — lawful acquisition and lawful use are separate questions. And “the customer agreed to the terms in 2019” does not automatically cover what you are doing now, if what you are doing now is materially different from what those terms described. Building a churn model on transaction data collected to run an account is exactly the sort of case where that needs an answer rather than an assumption.

Data controllers and processors of major importance. If you process personal data at volume, or you operate in a sector the Commission designates — financial services, communications, health, and oil and gas among them — you are likely in scope. Being in scope brings concrete obligations: registering with the Commission, appointing a Data Protection Officer, and filing compliance audit returns. GAID tiers this classification rather than applying one flat threshold, and the tier you land in determines what you file and what it costs. That is why it is worth resisting the single headline number you will find in a summary article — this page included — and asking your DPO which tier your organisation actually sits in.

The audit cycle. A compliance audit and an annual return, with filing fees under GAID running from ₦100,000 to ₦1,000,000 depending on the tier, from the 2026 cycle. Confirm the current filing window with your DPO — it is a fixed date in the calendar rather than a rolling anniversary of when you registered.

Data protection impact assessment (s.28). Where processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, you must carry out a DPIA. In plain terms a DPIA is a written analysis done before the processing starts: what you intend to do, why it is necessary, what could go wrong for the people whose data it is, and what you have put in place to reduce that. And where the DPIA concludes the processing would be high risk, you must consult the Commission before you process.

This is the obligation an analytics function is most likely to trip and least likely to have heard of, and the reason is understandable — it sits in data protection law rather than anywhere an analytics team would naturally look. Systematic profiling of individuals is squarely within it, and a churn score, an attrition score and a credit propensity are all systematic profiling of individuals. So: a model card is good practice. A DPIA may be the law. They are different documents written for different readers, and doing the first does not discharge the second.

Breach notification: 72 hours from discovery to the NDPC. There is a subtlety here that is easy and expensive to miss. As drafted, the trigger for notifying the Commission is a breach likely to result in a risk to rights and freedoms; the higher high risk threshold is what obliges you to notify the affected data subjects. Those are two different bars, and the failure mode is not deviousness — it is an incident room under pressure collapsing them into one question, deciding the breach is not serious enough to tell customers about, and concluding from that that it need not be reported to the Commission either. Keep a breach register, with the two questions asked separately and both answers recorded.

Cross-border transfer requires an adequate level of protection, demonstrated through the recipient country’s law, binding corporate rules, standard contractual clauses or an approved certification — and under GAID, instruments such as binding corporate rules require the Commission’s approval. Absent adequacy, transfer is still permitted on explicit informed consent, or for contract performance, vital interests, legal claims or the data subject’s direct benefit. The practical reading is that a route almost always exists, but it has to be chosen and documented in advance rather than reconstructed afterwards.

Automated decision-making. Data subjects have protection against decisions made solely by automated means, together with a right to human review. The word doing the work is “solely”, which is why the quality of the human review matters so much — a point the model card section returns to.

Penalties. For an entity of major importance, up to ₦10 million or 2% of annual gross revenue, whichever is higher. For others, ₦2 million or 2%. Non-compliance with a Commission order can carry imprisonment of up to one year. The figures are worth knowing, though in practice the disruption of an investigation and the reputational cost tend to dwarf the fine.

The question that applies to everything you learned this week

When you paste customer text into a hosted AI assistant, that is a processing operation, and if the model is hosted outside Nigeria it is very likely a cross-border transfer of personal data.

It needs a lawful basis and a transfer mechanism, in advance, in writing.

That lands uncomfortably at the end of a week spent doing exactly this sort of thing, so it is worth being clear about what it does and does not mean. It is not a reason to abandon the techniques in 4.1. It is the reason the local-model option appeared in that session rather than being tucked into a compliance annex at the end of the course. A model running on your own machine, processing your own data, transfers nothing anywhere — there is no recipient, no border and no mechanism to arrange — and for most theme-classification work the accuracy gap between a local model and a hosted one is smaller than the gap between two conscientious human labellers reading the same reviews.

The practical rule for Monday: anonymised or synthetic data, or shapes and schemas rather than values, may go to a hosted assistant. Actual customer records go to a local model, or to an enterprise deployment your organisation has procured and assessed, or nowhere. Most of the work you want to do fits comfortably in the first category, once you are in the habit of asking which category you are in.

The three questions, answered as a decision tree

The tree below is not a substitute for the analysis above; it is a way of getting to the right conversation quickly, for the routine cases. Run it while the work is being scoped, so that the answer shapes the design rather than arriving to challenge it.

Wednesday’s model comes back

On Wednesday several tables built an attrition model on hr_attrition.csv, and commute_minutes was among the strongest predictors. Nothing about that is surprising — how far somebody travels to work is a real and reasonable influence on whether they stay.

But consider what else that column carries. Commute time is a close proxy for where somebody lives; where somebody lives, in Lagos as in most large cities, is a close proxy for income; and in many markets residential geography also tracks ethnicity or religion. So a model with no protected attribute anywhere in its inputs — and there is none anywhere in that file — can nonetheless sort people substantially along lines the law cares about, because it has been handed a variable that quietly encodes them.

This is not a trap laid for the unwary. It is the ordinary situation, because useful predictors are useful precisely by being correlated with a lot of things about a person’s life.

The sentence to take away from Friday

Removing protected attributes from a dataset does not remove discrimination from a model. It removes your ability to measure it.

That is worth reading twice, because the intuition runs the other way: dropping the sensitive column feels like the responsible act, and it is often done in good faith by people trying to do the right thing. What it actually achieves is to leave the proxies in place while destroying the only column you could have used to check whether the model was treating groups differently.

So “is this variable allowed?” turns out to be the wrong question. Four better ones:

  1. What decision is this score attached to? A retention offer is a benefit — the worst outcome for somebody the model overlooks is that they do not get a call. A promotion or redundancy score is a detriment, and detriment decisions carry a far higher standard of evidence, documentation and review.
  2. Who bears the cost of being wrong? If the institution bears it, an error is a business cost. If the individual bears it — the employee not shortlisted, the customer refused — the bar rises again, because they did not choose the exposure and cannot see it.
  3. Can the affected person find out and contest it? If there is no route by which somebody learns a score was applied to them and no route by which they can challenge it, you do not have a governed model, however thorough the documentation.
  4. Would you defend this in front of the person it was applied to? Not to a regulator — to the employee, in a room, in ordinary words. It is a more demanding test than the compliance one and a considerably more useful one, because it catches the cases that are technically permitted and obviously wrong.

The honest resolution for the HR case is usually this: use the model to identify teams and conditions where attrition risk is elevated — commute burden, long waits for promotion, excessive span of control — and act on those systemically, by changing the conditions. Do not attach a score to an individual employee’s file.

That trade costs less than people fear. Nearly all the value in an attrition model is in knowing where and why the risk concentrates, which is what tells you what to change. Almost all the exposure is in the individual score, which is the part that gets applied to a person, sits in a file, and is very hard to explain to them. Giving up the second while keeping the first is not a compromise so much as a better use of the model.

The model card

A model card is one page per model, written in language a non-specialist can read, that records what the model is for and what it is not for. It takes about twenty minutes if you kept the comments from 2.6, and it is the artefact that makes everything else — the DPIA, the audit return, the answer to a data subject — possible to assemble later.

Model card — the fields that matter

Name
Retail churn propensity, v1.2
Owner
Named individual, plus the business owner who acts on it
Purpose
Rank customers for the monthly retention call list. Not for pricing, credit or service tiering.
Decision
Top 600 by expected value at risk are contacted. Benefit decision, not detriment.
Training data
12,000 customers, Jan 2024 – Mar 2026, one row per customer at snapshot
Features
Listed. Each with a note on when it becomes available in the business process.
Excluded, and why
closure_form_started — post-outcome, leaks. branch_state — geographic proxy, no business justification for the ranking.
Performance
AUC 0.77 on held-out data. Top-decile lift 3.0x. Baseline churn 10.6%.
Performance by segment
By segment, age band and state. Report the worst group, not the average.
Known limitations
Not validated on customers with under 3 months tenure. Not tested since the November re-segmentation.
Human review
Retention lead reviews the list; any customer may be removed with a logged reason.
Monitoring
Monthly: score distribution drift, feature drift, realised churn among contacted vs holdout.
Retire by
Review date, after which it must be revalidated or switched off.

Two of those lines are the ones organisations reliably leave blank, and it is worth understanding why each matters.

“Not for” exists because models are reused. A churn propensity built to order a call list is a plausible-looking input to a pricing decision, a service-tier decision or a credit decision, and eighteen months later somebody who was not in the room when it was built will find it in the warehouse and use it for one of those. They will not be behaving badly; the model is right there and it appears to answer their question. The “Not for” line is the only thing standing between that person and a decision the model was never validated for.

“Retire by” exists because models decay quietly. The world the model learned moves — a re-segmentation, a new product, a change in how a channel is used — and nothing in the pipeline announces that the model has become less accurate. A date forces the question to be asked on a schedule rather than after an incident, and it makes switching a model off a routine administrative act rather than an admission that something went wrong.

The “Human review” line deserves the same scrutiny, because given the protection against decisions made solely by automated means, this is the field where you establish that the decision is not solely automated. It needs to describe what the reviewer actually does, what they see, and what proportion of cases they change. A reviewer who approves a list of 600 in four minutes has not reviewed anything, and writing the field honestly is the point at which that becomes visible.

Your AI-use policy — one page, drafted this afternoon

The policy that works is not a forty-page document with a version history and no readers. It is one page, seven lines, specific to your function, written so that somebody joining next month could follow it without asking. Length is not thoroughness here; a policy nobody has read governs nothing.

  • What may go into a hosted assistant. Name the categories. Be concrete: “aggregated figures, schemas, anonymised extracts, public documents — yes. Customer records, employee records, unredacted contracts — no.”
  • What must stay local. And which tool people should use for it, installed and working.
  • Who decides the edge cases, and how long they take to respond. A policy with no fast route for exceptions gets routed around.
  • What gets logged. At minimum: what was used, on what data, for what decision.
  • Where a human must be in the loop, and what that human is actually expected to do. “Reviewed by a human” who rubber-stamps a list of 600 is not human review.
  • How somebody affected finds out and contests it.
  • When this policy is next reviewed. Put a date on it.

The second and third lines decide whether the policy survives contact with a deadline. If what must stay local has no working local tool behind it, the policy is asking people to choose between the rule and the work, and the work wins. If edge cases go into a queue with no stated response time, people stop raising them — not defiantly, but because they have something due on Thursday. Both are failures of provisioning rather than of character, which is good news, because provisioning is something you control.

Core lab · 20 minutes

  • Run the decision tree on your capstone, write down which branch you land on, and answer in writing: does it need a DPIA? If it scores individuals, probably yes — name who would sign it.
  • Write the model card for the model you built this week. Eight minutes, and the two fields that matter are Not for and Retire by. Include performance on its worst segment, not its average.
  • Draft the seven-line AI-use policy for your function.
  • Sceptic: find the line in their policy that their own team will route around, and say why.

The Sceptic’s task is the sharpest one on the list, and it is meant kindly. Every policy has a line that will be quietly bypassed, usually because it makes a common task slower without offering a faster compliant route. Finding it now, from a colleague, costs nothing. Finding it in an audit costs a great deal more.

Stretch

Write the fairness section of your model card properly: performance broken down by every group you can lawfully measure, with the worst group named explicitly rather than averaged into the headline.

Then answer, in writing, the question the panel may ask this afternoon: “if this model is 6 points less accurate for one group, what happens to the people in that group, and is that acceptable?”

There is no formula that answers that, and the absence is not a gap in the method. Whether a gap is acceptable depends on what the score is used for, what the alternative process would have done, and who carries the cost of the errors — three things that no metric contains and that the person accountable for the decision has to weigh. That is precisely why the question sits with the person in this room rather than with the model, and why being able to answer it in your own words is worth more than any amount of documentation.

Takeaways

  1. Governance is where programmes get stopped. Do it in week one, not month seven.
  2. Lawful basis before processing. 72 hours for a high-risk breach. Cross-border transfer needs a mechanism, in writing, in advance.
  3. Pasting customer text into a hosted assistant is very likely a cross-border transfer. The local model is the practical answer.
  4. Removing protected attributes removes your ability to measure discrimination, not the discrimination.
  5. A model card is good practice; a DPIA may be a legal obligation, and profiling individuals is squarely inside it. They are different documents.
  6. One model card per model. The fields nobody fills in are “Not for” and “Retire by”.
  7. A one-page policy people follow beats a forty-page one they route around.
